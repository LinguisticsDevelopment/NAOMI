## 0. Executive summary (for the project owner)

1. The space works for "how alike are two words" (synonym AUC ~0.88, hypernymy ~0.72). It fails at two things the new scorer exposed: it cannot separate opposites, and it cannot cleanly separate unrelated words.
2. The causes are mechanical, not conceptual: three prime axes sit on ~98% of senses, senses carry no sign at all (0 negative values in 2.79M entries), and signs on core words depend on an English cue-word list.
3. The literature has two broad ideas for opposites: put each opposite pair on its own two-ended axis (semantic differential, SemAxis, POLAR), or keep opposition as a relation and consult it when comparing (what the project does now). Both are old; neither is free.
4. WordNet already contains the raw material for two-ended axes. MEASURED-NOW: 3,846 head adjective synsets carry a direct antonym pointer; they form ~2,000 head-to-head pairs; 14,532 of 18,156 adjective synsets (80%) sit in such bipolar clusters; the other ~3,600 are almost all "pertainym" adjectives (relational, like "atomic") with no opposite.
5. The catch is the number of axes: ~2,000 raw pair-axes is far from "minimum". Pair-axes would have to be merged (e.g., via WordNet's own `attribute` links: 620 adjective heads have one) or limited to a smaller core. Merging is the unresolved design question.
6. Verbs, nouns and adverbs barely have antonym pointers (996 verb synsets, 1,922 noun synsets, 630 adverb synsets). Two-ended axes alone will therefore cover adjectives well and everything else weakly.
7. Opposites come in different kinds (alive/dead cannot be "more or less"; buy/sell are two views of one event; tie/untie reverse an action). A single sign on a single axis fits only the gradable kind. This agrees with the owner's own caveat.
8. Taxonomy-based coordinates (is-a paths, information content) would help noun pairs like dog/cat vs. unrelated nouns, and verbs are shallow (mean depth 2.5), so eat/chew gets little. They give nothing for adjectives: 0 of 18,156 have a hypernym.
9. Human-rated norms (VAD, Lancaster sensorimotor, Binder 65 attributes, THINGS dimensions) give few, named, human-checked dimensions, and VAD is signed. But they cover words not senses, are mostly licensed for research only (non-redistribution for NRC VAD), and some exceed "derived from dictionaries" in a way only the owner can rule on.
10. Methods that inject antonymy into vectors (retrofitting, counter-fitting, Attract-Repel, SPINE, sparse coding) are the best-evidenced ones, but they learn per-word values or create unnamed dimensions: they fail requirements 2 and 3 as stated. They are useful as an evaluation baseline, not as the design.
11. How many dimensions does meaning need? Semantic differential: 3 (evaluation, potency, activity). Interpretable object similarity: 49 (THINGS). Binder: 65 hand-chosen. Lancaster: 11. Embeddings: intrinsic dimension ~10-30. The project's own estimate: ~17 effective, ~46 for 90% energy. A named, non-rotated set is necessarily redundant versus a rotated one, so "minimal" can realistically mean "no useless axes", not "the smallest possible rank".
12. Cheap first step with no downside: drop or down-weight the ubiquitous axes (IDF/pruning). It is reversible and is a precondition for any sign-based fix to show up in cosine.
13. The design that looks structurally closest to the owner's intuition is "typed hybrid": category axes (what kind of thing), scale axes (two-ended, signed, from WordNet clusters), and operator axes (closed-class only). It is also the most work.
14. The project has already tried: gloss-cue signs on attribute axes (kept, 0.40 -> 0.69), pole pairs on three prime axes (not adopted), per-word trained values (closed), sense-node propagation (closed). Pair-axes from WordNet clusters as a placement device were NOT tried (history s2.3, row "Antonym-pair axes").
15. Evaluation sets exist for exactly the weaknesses seen: SimLex-999 (similarity vs relatedness), WordSim-353 split, SimVerb-3500, HyperLex, BLESS, the Nguyen et al. antonym/synonym set (15,642 pairs), the GRE "most contrasting word" set. All are free to download for research; some licences are unstated.
16. Any change that makes senses signed breaks a recorded invariant (senses non-negative, history s8.1 item 8) and changes the pinned fingerprint; this should be an additive layer or a new version, not an edit.
17. Pole choice is the hard part of two-ended axes: for hot/cold either end can be "+". Orientation does not matter inside one axis but matters if axes are later merged; a data-derived rule (e.g., the more frequent member is the "+" end) exists but is a convention.
18. Cross-language: WordNet antonym pointers sit on lemmas, but head-to-head bipolar cluster membership sits on synsets, so synset-level opposition could transfer through sense identifiers (INFERENCE; needs a check on Open Multilingual Wordnet).
19. Five questions (section 7) decide most of this: is human-rated data acceptable, may one axis hold both a meaning position and be acted on by operator words, how many axes is "minimum", is a signed sense layer allowed, and what accuracy gain is worth a rebuild.
20. Suggested order, if the owner wants to proceed: run the read-only probes in section 8 first (a day of work, no build), then decide.

### A. Keep the space; handle opposition and discrimination outside it

**How it works.** Leave coordinates alone. (a) Consult the antonym relation when comparing two nodes (what `score.py` already does: recorded opposites count 0). (b) Reweight axes at comparison time: IDF
or entropy weights so ubiquitous axes count little (already used by `usvs_graded`, M30). (c) Prune axes: drop axes that are on nearly all senses or on almost none, or rank by leave-one-out contribution (the
honest-minimality procedure of M20.1, never re-run on 607 axes, history s6).

**References.** Retrieval-style IDF is standard (no specific citation needed). Widdows (2003) "Orthogonal negation in vector spaces for modelling word-meanings and document retrieval", ACL 2003, pp. 136-143 [V] is the
classical "remove a meaning component" idea; Mohammad, Dorr, Hirst, Turney (2013) "Computing Lexical Contrast", Computational Linguistics 39(3):555-590 [V] reports that more than 90% of GRE "most contrasting word"
pairs are NOT listed as WordNet antonyms [V], i.e., a pointer store alone under-covers human-judged opposites.

**Data / open / multilingual.** All internal. IDF needs only the artifact. Antonym relation: WordNet lemma pointers (open; lemma-level, mostly not carried into other-language wordnets, INFERENCE).

**Dimensions / coverage.** 607 -> fewer if pruned (project record: honest fidelity peaked at ~80-120 axes on a 234-axis space, history s6; not measured on 607). Coverage of the opposition fix is exactly the pointer coverage: 3,992 lemma pairs,
~11k with tiers.

**Opposites / near-synonyms / unrelated / hypernyms / polysemy.** Opposites: separated only where a recorded edge exists, so happy/sad (not a recorded pair) stays ~0.85. Near-synonyms: unchanged. Unrelated: improves with IDF/pruning (M42: IDF 50/62 -> 53/62; M21's sparse
form took random similarity 0.32 -> 0.03). Hypernyms: unchanged. Polysemy: lemma-level edges inherit sense conflation (history s2.4, M29 caveat).

**Fit.** R1 partial (pruning moves toward it); R2 pass; R3 pass; R4 partial (edges are lemma-level); R5 fail (opposition stays a relation; this is the owner's intuition's opposite); R6 pass; R7 pass (lowest risk).

**History.** This is the current position (history s2.5, RN 1489-1494). New within it: actually re-running minimality on 607 axes, and applying IDF/pruning inside the stored vectors (not only at query time).

**Risks.** Leaves the owner's stated intuition unmet; relation store has no live consumer except the scorer; coverage ceiling (>90% of human contrast pairs are not WordNet antonyms [V]).

**Probe (read-only).** From `data/usvs/`: (1) compute for each axis its sense frequency and IDF; recompute the scorer's example pairs (dog/cat vs eat/chew, happy/unhappy/sad, unrelated pairs) with the top-k most ubiquitous axes zeroed, for k = 0, 3, 10, 30; (2) run the leave-one-out contribution ranking on the 607 axes using the held-out harness; (3) report how many of SimLex-999's lowest-scored pairs are WordNet antonym pairs.

### B. Signed attribute scales at the sense level

**How it works.** Carry the existing `attr:` sign down to senses and remove the cue-word list. Today only core words get signed `attr:` values (gloss-cue sign, then propagation); senses are rebuilt as non-negative blends. Variant B1: store sign in sense vectors for attribute axes (schema change). Variant B2: derive the sign from data
instead of cue words: for an antonym head pair that shares an attribute noun (e.g., hot/cold -> temperature) orient the two heads +/-; satellites inherit via `similar_to`; orientation fixed by a data convention (e.g., the member with the higher WordNet/SemCor tag count is "+", linguistic markedness) rather than words like "high"/"low".

**References.** Sheinman, Fellbaum, Julien, Schulam, Tokunaga (2013) "Large, huge or gigantic? Identifying and encoding intensity relations among adjectives in WordNet", Language Resources and Evaluation 47(3):797-816 [V] adds an intensity relation to WordNet dumbbells (ordered scales per attribute) mined from web patterns: relevant for graded position along a scale, but it is a separate resource and web-derived. Gross & Miller (1990) "Adjectives in WordNet", Int. J. of Lexicography 3(4) [V-s: authorship and issue verified; page claims not]: the head/satellite bipolar organisation. Kennedy & McNally (2005) "Scale structure, degree modification, and the semantics of gradable predicates", Language 81(2):345-381 [V]: scales may be open or closed; closed-scale adjectives (full/empty, open/closed) behave differently from open-scale ones (tall/short).

**Data / open / multilingual.** WordNet `attribute`, `antonym`, `similar_to` (open). Sense-level sign is carried by synset identity, so the sign layer itself is language-neutral once synset-level (not lemma-level) opposition is used (INFERENCE). Tag counts for orientation come from SemCor counts shipped with WordNet (English only; INFERENCE that other languages would need another convention or none, since orientation is arbitrary).

**Dimensions / coverage.** No new axes: the existing 498 `attr:` axes (21 have 0 senses, 42 <=50 senses, history s6). Coverage: only adjectives with an `attribute` pointer (620 heads, 940 synsets incl. nouns) plus their satellites via `similar_to`; MEASURED-NOW (history s2.2) only 414 of 9,946 core words have an attribute relation at all. Verbs/nouns: essentially none.

**Opposites.** Pairs that share an attribute noun get opposite signs: hot/cold, big/small if WordNet links them. happy/unhappy: WordNet's `attribute` for happy is not standard (INFERENCE: check; many evaluative adjectives have no attribute pointer, which is why quality/good/bad was special-cased by prime pole axes). Near-synonyms: same sign, high cosine. Unrelated: unchanged, since sign adds no discrimination there. Hypernyms: unchanged. Polysemy: sign is per sense, which is an improvement over today's per-word sign.

**Fit.** R1 pass (no new axes); R2 pass; R3 pass for B2 (orientation convention must be stated), fail-ish for B1 if cue words stay; R4 partial/pass; R5 partial (only attribute-linked adjectives); R6 pass; R7 pass (small, testable).

**History.** Extends M19.2 (anchored sign, 0.693 held-out) and removes M18.1's cue lists. Different from what failed: M18.1/M21 collapsed three prime pole axes or operated per word; here the sign is per sense on existing attribute axes and propagated by existing edges. Honest expectation (history s2.2): the cue list was worth only ~0.04; a better sign source cannot be expected to give a big gain on its own.

**Risks.** Breaks "senses are non-negative" (history s8.1 #8) for every consumer (`encoder_model.py:236`, `usvs_graded.py:255`); fingerprint change; attribute coverage is only ~8% of words / 35% of adjectives (RN 1224); orientation convention; sign vs magnitude confusion on non-gradable pairs.

**Probe.** Count, over WordNet: adjective head pairs that share an `attribute` noun (hot/cold-type) vs. those that do not (good/bad, happy/unhappy-type); fraction of the 3,846 antonym heads reachable; orientation rule consistency (does the tag-count rule give the same sign for the same attribute across independent pairs?); then compute the sign agreement with the existing gloss-cue signs for the 130 words that have them (a free consistency check: if the data rule recovers the cue-list signs, the list can go).

### C. Antonym-pair scales as dimensions (semantic differential family)

**How it works.** Each antonym pair (or each WordNet adjective cluster) defines a two-ended axis; other words are placed by their relation to the two poles (nearer pole, signed projection). Three concrete versions exist in the literature:
- **Semantic differential** (Osgood, Suci, Tannenbaum 1957, *The Measurement of Meaning*, Univ. of Illinois Press [V]): people rate concepts on bipolar adjective scales; factor analysis repeatedly yields three general factors, evaluation, potency, activity [V-s via the 1975 cross-cultural book and encyclopedia summaries; the 1957 book's own chapter on dimensionality (p. 31) was not read]. Osgood, May, Miron (1975), *Cross-Cultural Universals of Affective Meaning* [V-s].
- **SemAxis** (An, Kwak, Ahn, ACL 2018, "SemAxis: A Lightweight Framework to Characterize Domain-Specific Word Semantics Beyond Sentiment") [V]: an axis is the difference between the average vectors of two pole word sets; 732 axes built from ConceptNet antonym pairs; a word is scored by cosine with the axis. FrameAxis (Kwak, An, Jing, Ahn, PeerJ Computer Science 7:e644, 2021) [V]: antonym-pair "microframes" give bias and intensity of a text on each axis. Both sit on pretrained embeddings: the poles are named, the geometry underneath is learned.
- **POLAR** (Mathew, Sikdar, Lemmerich, Strohmaier, WWW 2020, pp. 1548-1558) [V]: transforms existing embeddings into a space whose dimensions are polar-opposite pairs taken from WordNet-style sources; post-processing, interpretable by construction, performance preserved per the authors [V from abstract].
- **WordNet adjective organisation** (above): head synsets with direct antonym pointers, satellites attached by similar_to; "dumbbell" structure; relational (pertainym) adjectives are outside it [V-s].
- **Conceptual spaces** (Gärdenfors 2000, *Conceptual Spaces: The Geometry of Thought*, MIT Press [V-s]): quality dimensions grouped into domains, some with natural zero or two ends; this supplies the "domain + scale" language used in H.

**Data / open / multilingual.** WordNet (open); ConceptNet 5.5 (Speer, Chin, Havasi, AAAI 2017 [V]; open, multilingual, antonym edges). For WordNet-only construction (no embeddings) the data are internal and deterministic. Cross-lingual: bipolar cluster membership is synset-level (INFERENCE: transferable), lemma antonym pointers are not (INFERENCE).

**Dimensions / coverage (MEASURED-NOW).** Raw: 1,999 head-head antonymous pairs (a ceiling on pair-axes from adjectives); 3,992 lemma antonym pairs overall. Adjective coverage 80% of adjective synsets (14,532/18,156) lie in clusters; adjectives are ~15% of all synsets (nouns 70%, verbs 12%, adverbs 3%), so
coverage of the whole sense inventory is ~12% by direct cluster membership plus whatever propagation adds. Verb/noun/adverb antonym pointers: 996 / 1,922 / 630 synsets. Merging pair-axes into fewer dimensions is the open step (candidates: merge by shared attribute noun; by `also_see`/similar links between clusters; by lexicographer file; each is a data rule, each needs checking).

**Opposites / synonyms / unrelated / hypernyms / polysemy.** Opposites: separated by construction on their shared axis (opposite signs) and by nothing else. Near-synonyms: same side of the same axis. Unrelated words: placed on different axes, so cosine ~0 (good for the random-pair floor) provided no ubiquitous dimension shares mass. Hypernyms: no help (adjectives have no hypernyms). Polysemy: a word that sits in two clusters (hot = temperature and = spicy/stolen/popular) gets mass on two axes; that is right at sense level and wrong at word level (project's core coords are word-level).
**Known failure modes.** (1) Words related to both ends (e.g., "lukewarm", "fine", "okay"): near the axis origin or on a neighbour axis; pole assignment by "nearer pole" is unstable there. (2) Non-gradable/complementary opposites (alive/dead, open/shut): the axis exists but intermediate values are meaningless (Kennedy & McNally closed scales [V]). (3) Converses (buy/sell, give/receive, husband/wife) and reversives (tie/untie, rise/fall): not two ends of a scale but two perspectives or a direction of change; Cruse (1986) *Lexical Semantics* [V-s] distinguishes complementaries, antonyms (polar, overlapping, equipollent), directional opposites (antipodals, counterparts, reversives) and relational opposites/converses. (4) Multiple opposites for one word ("big" vs "little" vs "small" vs "tiny"): handled by clusters, not pairs. (5) Embedding-based versions (SemAxis, POLAR) inherit all corpus artifacts of the embedding, and a recorded observation about contrast measures: pole words co-occur, so distributional axes can put antonyms close (Mohammad et al. 2013 [V] motivates thesaurus-structure use for this reason).

**Fit.** R1 fail/partial (raw ~2,000 axes unless merged); R2 pass (named by their pole words) for WordNet-only construction, partial for SemAxis/POLAR (named but over a learned basis); R3 pass for WordNet-only, fail for POLAR/SemAxis (pretrained embeddings are trained); R4 partial; R5 pass, within gradable adjectives only; R6 pass (operators stay separate; but see owner question O2); R7 partial: untested as a placement device here.

**History.** Not tried as a placement device (history s2.3). Closest relatives that failed or were not adopted: M18.1 signed poles on three prime axes (0.466), M21 words as signed values on shared axes with Null mask (0.48), M25 trained values. What would be different: one axis PER cluster (not shared), signs from WordNet pointers (not cue words), values assigned to senses, ubiquitous axes removed so the sign is not drowned.

**Risks.** Axis-count explosion; incompatible with the "minimal set" goal unless merged; each merge is a hand-judgment unless purely data-driven; poor coverage outside adjectives; loses the clean prime-basis story (primes become one family among many); interactions with M20's finding that "the last ~100 axes hurt".

**Probe.** From WordNet only: (1) build the 1,999 head-pair graph; merge by attribute noun and by also_see/similar edges; report the number of connected components, the size distribution, and how many axes cover 90% of in-cluster adjective senses (a power-law tail would suggest a small core); (2) for each cluster report whether both poles are populated and how many satellites are on each side; (3) list the 100 largest clusters and hand-inspect 20 for coherence (happy/unhappy, good/bad, hot/cold, fast/slow...); (4) simulate: signed placement of adjective senses on cluster axes, then measure syn>ant AUC on the Nguyen et al. set restricted to adjectives and on SimLex adjectives.

### D. Feature-norm and property-based interpretable spaces

**How it works.** Use human-rated attributes as the named dimensions: each word/concept gets a vector of rating values on a small set of named properties. Sources:
- **McRae, Cree, Seidenberg, McNorgan (2005)**, Behavior Research Methods 37(4):547-559 [V]: feature production norms, 541 concrete concepts, ~725 participants [V]; a later featural semantic space of 2,526 dimensions is described in secondary sources [V-s]. Too small for a lexicon, and features are free-text properties, not a compact named set.
- **Binder et al. (2016)**, "Toward a brain-based componential semantic representation", Cognitive Neuropsychology 33(3-4):130-174 [V]: ~65 experiential attributes (sensory, motor, spatial, temporal, affective, social, cognitive) rated for 535 English words [V-s; the lab page lists "65-dimensional attribute ratings for 535 English words", another paper says 68 features: unresolved]. Open download from the lab page [V-s]. From memory several attributes split poles into separate unsigned scales (e.g., large vs small, fast vs slow, pleasant vs unpleasant) [U]; if true this is the same pattern as NSM's BIG and SMALL axes.
- **Lancaster Sensorimotor Norms** (Lynott, Connell, Brysbaert, Brand, Carney 2020), Behavior Research Methods 52(3):1271-1291 [V]: 39,707 concepts rated on six perceptual modalities (touch, hearing, smell, taste, vision, interoception) and five action effectors (11 dimensions); data on OSF; licence stated inconsistently across repositories (CC BY vs CC BY-NC) [V-s; check the OSF page]. Strengths, not directions: unsigned.
- **Affective norms:** Warriner, Kuperman, Brysbaert (2013) 13,915 lemmas, Behavior Research Methods 45(4):1191-1207 [V]; Mohammad (2018) NRC VAD, 20,000 words, ACL 2018 pp. 174-184 [V]; NRC VAD v2: >55,000 English terms and machine translations into ~108 languages, free for research, no redistribution, translations sometimes wrong [V from the project page via search]. Valence is signed (negative to positive): the only family in D where happy/unhappy fall on opposite ends of a named dimension by data.
- **THINGS / SPoSE:** Hebart, Zheng, Pereira, Baker (2020), Nature Human Behaviour 4(11):1173-1185 [V]: 49 human-interpretable, non-negative dimensions from 1.46M odd-one-out triplets over 1,854 objects [V-s for the count of triplets and the name SPoSE, which the abstract search did not state]. Data on OSF (licence not checked) [V-s].
- **Conceptual spaces** (Gärdenfors 2000 [V-s]) gives the philosophy (quality dimensions with domains) rather than a dataset.

**Data / open / multilingual.** Human-rated, per word (not per sense), almost all English; NRC VAD has automatic translations; Lancaster has some non-English counterparts (not checked, [U]). Licences range from open to research-only. Whether ratings count as "data" under R3 is owner question O1.

**Dimensions / coverage.** 3 (VAD), 11 (Lancaster), ~65 (Binder), 49 (THINGS) vs 607 now. Coverage: Lancaster/VAD tens of thousands of words but not senses and not closed-class words; Binder/McRae/THINGS hundreds to ~1,800 words. To cover 117,659 senses the values would have to be propagated through WordNet exactly like current attr values (deterministic), which is R3-compatible but adds a propagation step on rated seeds. Rated seeds are not senses: each word-level rating would have to be assigned to a sense (first sense? all senses? both are approximations, INFERENCE).

**Opposites / synonyms / unrelated / hypernyms / polysemy.** Opposites: separated on valence (VAD) for evaluative pairs only; hot/cold, big/small, fast/slow not separated unless the attribute set has directional scales (Lancaster: no, strength only). Near-synonyms: close. Unrelated: well separated on perceptual/affective profile for concrete words, poorly for abstract. Hypernyms: only through shared profile. Polysemy: word-level ratings blur senses.

**Fit.** R1 pass (few dims) -- the strongest family on R1; R2 pass (dimension names are the rating scales); R3 partial (data-derived and deterministic once propagated, but human-rated rather than from a dictionary: O1; no trained values); R4 partial/fail (rated in one language; translations automatic); R5 partial (valence only, plus whichever other signed scales the norm set provides); R6 pass; R7 partial: uncertain gain for abstract words and function-adjacent vocabulary.

**History.** Not tried. The project's own `attr:` axes are the WordNet analogue of Binder-style properties, but data-derived. Valence has appeared only as GOOD/BAD prime axes and as the owner-removed appraisal node (D2).

**Risks.** Licences (non-redistribution of NRC VAD would prevent shipping coordinates derived from it, INFERENCE from the stated terms); small coverage of abstract/relational vocabulary; word-vs-sense mismatch; adds an English-centric human data dependency to an otherwise lexical pipeline.

**Probe.** Download-free first: compute the overlap between each norm's word list and the 9,946 core words and 117,659 senses' lemmas (counts only). Then correlate each rated dimension with the existing USVS axes on the overlap (is "valence" already recoverable from `attr:quality` and GOOD/BAD? if cosine/regression R^2 is high, the dimension is redundant, if low, it is new information). Report which words have rating but no WordNet sense (fixes cannot propagate).

### E. Taxonomy-derived coordinates

**How it works.** Place a sense by its position in the is-a hierarchy. Similarity measures are classical: Resnik (1995), "Using information content to evaluate semantic similarity in a taxonomy", IJCAI-95 pp. 448-452 [V] (similarity = information content of the most specific common ancestor; reported r = 0.79 with WordNet + Brown frequencies per a secondary summary [V-s]); Lin (1998), "An information-theoretic definition of similarity", ICML pp. 296-304 [V; formula not read]; Wu & Palmer (1994), "Verb semantics and lexical selection", ACL 32, pp. 133-138 [V] (depth of the lowest common subsumer); Seco, Veale, Hayes (2004), intrinsic information content from structure alone, ECAI pp. 1089-1090 [V]. These are comparison measures, not coordinates. To make coordinates, one uses ancestor indicators (a sense is a vector over the named ancestor synsets at chosen depths) or "domain axes" such as the project's own 44 `lex:` categories.
Learned geometric versions: Poincare embeddings (Nickel & Kiela, NeurIPS 2017) [V] and box embeddings / box lattice (Vilnis, Li, Murty, McCallum, ACL 2018, pp. 263-272) [V]. Both are trained and produce unnamed dimensions (the box version has box corners, not named ones): R2 and R3 fail as stated. (A deterministic hyperbolic placement of a tree exists in the literature, Sarkar 2011 [U]; it would still have unnamed coordinates.)

**Data / open / multilingual.** WordNet hypernym graph (open); other wordnets via synset ids (OMW: Bond & Foster 2013, "Linking and extending an open multilingual wordnet", ACL pp. 1352-1362 [V]: >26 languages, >2M senses, open licences). Hypernym structure transfers by synset id, which is a real cross-lingual advantage for this family.

**Dimensions / coverage (MEASURED-NOW).** Distinct noun ancestors at hierarchy depth index 2/3/4/5: 26 / 254 / 2,274 / 8,523 (so a "category axes at depth 3" set would be ~250 named axes; depth 4 already exceeds the whole current space). Coverage: nouns and verbs. Adjectives: 0 of 18,156 have a hypernym; adverbs none either. Verbs are shallow: 13,767 synsets, mean maximal depth 2.5, 559 root-level verb synsets. Example scores (Wu-Palmer, MEASURED-NOW): dog/cat 0.857; eat/chew 0.20 (eat.v.01 depth 2, chew.v.01 depth 5), so a plain path measure also makes dog/cat far closer than eat/chew: the weakness the scorer found would persist for verbs under this family.

**Opposites / synonyms / unrelated / hypernyms / polysemy.** Opposites: not represented; antonyms such as hot/cold are co-hyponyms-or-unrelated in the tree. Near-synonyms: same node. Unrelated: well separated for nouns (distant subtrees). Hypernyms: represented explicitly (the strength of this family: HyperLex/BLESS-type tests). Polysemy: handled by sense nodes (project closed this door as the primary space, M22-23: "sense-node propagation barely beats chance" for the close-edge version; that was propagation, not ancestor coordinates, so not the same experiment).

**Fit.** R1 partial (depth-3 cut = ~250 axes; deeper cuts blow up); R2 pass for ancestor axes (each axis is an actual synset), fail for learned embeddings; R3 pass for ancestor/IC, fail for Poincare/box; R4 pass (synset ids); R5 fail (no scales); R6 pass; R7 partial.

**History.** Hypernymy as a relation: 1,546 directed genus edges; M28.1: "Hypernymy, like antonymy, is directional structure -- belongs in the relational store, not closeness"; genus-differentia as a close edge: hypernym +0.010 but similar -0.021 and random +0.022 (negative). Domain features (lexicographer-like) added ~370 axes and helped random-pair separation (M28.1). What would be different: ancestors as axes (not propagation edges), chosen depth cut, and using this ONLY for nouns/verbs inside a typed hybrid (H).

**Risks.** Depth is not comparable across branches (WordNet is uneven); verbs are flat; no adjective/adverb story; ancestor axes are highly collinear (each ancestor implies its parent), so the effective dimension is not the axis count; extra axes again conflict with R1 and with "the last ~100 axes hurt".

**Probe.** From WordNet: for the 9,946 core words' first senses, compute the ancestor-set cosine at depths 3-5 and compare to the artifact's cosine on the scorer's examples and on the SimLex noun and verb subsets; report the fraction of core words with no ancestor information (adjectives, adverbs, function-adjacent); report effective rank of the ancestor matrix (participation ratio, as in the project's M19.1) to estimate how many independent axes it truly adds.

### F. Decompositional approaches (NSM explications, frames, qualia, ConceptNet)

**How it works.** Define meaning through explicit decomposition into smaller named parts.
- **NSM** (Wierzbicka; Goddard): 65 primes [V-s] plus "semantic molecules" [V-s: Goddard & Wierzbicka, *Words and Meanings*, OUP 2014; Goddard 2012/2016 on molecules]: non-primitive meanings (e.g., hands, water, long, children) used as building blocks in explications. The project already uses primes as seed axes. For gradable adjectives and antonyms I could NOT verify how NSM explicates them: the searches showed good/bad and big/small are primes (so their opposition is built into the prime set), and I found no source on NSM treatment of non-prime opposites (e.g., hot/cold, alive/dead) [U].
- **FrameNet** (Baker, Fillmore, Lowe, "The Berkeley FrameNet Project", COLING-ACL 1998, pp. 86-90) [V]: frames with named frame elements; a frame is a named, interpretable unit, but there are hundreds to ~1,200 frames, each organised around situations, not scales. [count U]
- **VerbNet** (Kipper-Schuler 2005, PhD thesis, Univ. of Pennsylvania) [V-s; thesis not retrieved]: verb classes (Levin-based) with thematic roles and mappings to WordNet, PropBank and FrameNet [V-s]. A class membership axis for verbs (a named category set) complements WordNet's shallow verb hierarchy.
- **Pustejovsky qualia** (*The Generative Lexicon*, MIT Press 1995) [V-s]: four roles (formal, constitutive, telic, agentive) per noun; a typed structure, not a set of scales, and no resource with broad coverage that is open and uniform [U for any such resource].
- **ConceptNet** (Speer, Chin, Havasi 2017 [V]): typed relations (IsA, HasProperty, Antonym, UsedFor, ...), multilingual; edges are crowd/derived and noisy.

**Data / open / multilingual.** FrameNet, VerbNet, ConceptNet open; frames have been built for other languages in separate projects [U]; ConceptNet is multilingual by construction.

**Dimensions / coverage.** Primes: 65 (already in). Frames: hundreds; VerbNet: a few hundred classes [U]; ConceptNet relation types: ~34 [U] but edges not axes. None gives a broad-coverage set of scale dimensions. Coverage of frames over WordNet senses: partial via the FrameNet-WordNet mappings [U].

**Opposites etc.** Frames/VerbNet: group verbs by situation, with no handling of opposites except where a frame has "Opposite"-type relations (not verified) [U]. NSM: opposition is partly primitive-level (GOOD/BAD, BIG/SMALL are primes) and otherwise requires explications; the explicit use of semantic molecules for adjectives was not found.

**Fit.** R1 partial; R2 pass for NSM/frames; R3 partial (explications are hand-written; hand-written explications are language resources, but writing them for 117k senses is not data-derived); R4 partial (NSM is explicitly cross-linguistic: its main selling point; frames partly); R5 partial (only primes); R6 pass; R7 low for the cost.

**History.** The project began here (M17 decomposed glosses to primes, then M46 "USVS is our Semantic Metalanguage, we don't need a vestigial lossy one" -- adopted on architectural grounds). Gloss-to-prime decomposition is now the OOV fallback (1,208 senses, 1.0%). FrameNet/VerbNet/qualia were not tried.

**Risks.** Hand effort scale; coverage; frames are not scales; verbs only for VerbNet.

**Probe.** Compute coverage of VerbNet classes and FrameNet LUs over the project's verb senses (counts only; if VerbNet class membership covers >80% of the 13,767 verb synsets via its WordNet mappings it could become a verb category axis set, otherwise skip); read the NSM publications on adjective/antonym explications and note exactly which opposites are primitives vs defined (closing the [U] above).

### G. Post-processing methods that inject antonymy/synonymy into vectors

**How it works.** Start from distributional vectors; move them so that linked words get closer and antonyms further.
- **Retrofitting** (Faruqui, Dodge, Jauhar, Dyer, Hovy, Smith, NAACL 2015, pp. 1606-1615) [V]: nudges vectors of lexicon-linked words together (WordNet, FrameNet, PPDB); synonyms/attract only.
- **Counter-fitting** (Mrksic et al., NAACL 2016, pp. 142-148) [V]: injects antonymy (repel) and synonymy (attract) constraints; reported new state of the art on SimLex-999 at the time [V].
- **Attract-Repel** (Mrksic, Vulic, O Seaghdha, Leviant, Reichart, Gasic, Korhonen, Young; TACL 5:309-324, 2017, titled "Semantic Specialization of Distributional Word Vector Spaces using Monolingual and Cross-Lingual Constraints") [V]: monolingual and cross-lingual constraints; six languages.
- **Lexical-contrast embeddings:** Ono, Miwa, Sasaki, NAACL 2015 pp. 984-989 [V bibliographic only]; Nguyen, Schulte im Walde, Vu, ACL 2016 short, pp. 454-459 [V]; AntSynNET (Nguyen et al., EACL 2017 pp. 76-85) [V].
- **Sparse/interpretable recodings:** Sparse overcomplete vectors, "SPOWV" per the task (Faruqui, Tsvetkov, Yogatama, Dyer, Smith, ACL 2015, pp. 1491-1500) [V]; SPINE (Subramanian et al., AAAI 2018) [V]; Non-negative sparse embedding NNSE (Murphy, Talukdar, Mitchell, COLING 2012, pp. 1933-1950) [V]. These produce sparse, partly human-interpretable (via intrusion tests) but unnamed dimensions. Interpretability scoring by categories: Senel et al. (2018), "Semantic structure and interpretability of word embeddings", IEEE/ACM TASLP 26(10):1769-1779, with the SEMCAT set of >6,500 words in 110 categories [V]. Hollis & Westbury (2016), Psychon. Bull. Rev. 23(6):1744-1756 [V bibliographic]: principal components of embeddings correlate with affect (valence/arousal/dominance) [V-s].

**Data / open / multilingual.** Need a trained embedding as input; constraints from WordNet/PPDB (open). Attract-Repel is explicitly cross-lingual.

**Dimensions.** Input dimensionality (e.g., 300) or a larger sparse space (SPINE/SPOWV thousands). Axes unnamed.

**Opposites etc.** Counter-fitting/Attract-Repel are the best-evidenced for separating antonyms from synonyms while keeping relatedness; SimLex gains are documented. Sparse recodings do not address antonymy.

**Fit.** R1 fail/partial; R2 fail (dimensions unnamed, rotated); R3 fail (trained embedding, learned per-word values, M25 reproduces this failure mode: "more training = worse"); R4 pass for Attract-Repel; R5 partial; R6 pass; R7 well-evidenced in the literature but evidence is on embeddings, not on this space.

**History.** Closest relatives: M21b/M25 (trained per-word values; closed doors). The M30 result that adding the signed antonym store lifted SimLex from 0.380 to 0.417 puts this project's two-part design near word2vec's ~0.41-0.44 range (RECORD); published counter-fitting numbers are higher but are on strong embeddings and unverified here [U for numbers].

**What could still be used.** Deterministic pieces: IDF-like reweighting; constraints as a data source; Attract-Repel/counter-fitting as an external baseline for evaluation only (what score do trained methods get on the same antonym/synonym sets as a ceiling?). SEMCAT-style category intrusion as an interpretability check for any new axis set (named categories in, check each axis). I found no method in this family that is deterministic and yields named axes.

**Risks.** Would reintroduce what the project closed (trained per-word values; M25).

**Probe.** None needed for the design. For calibration: run an off-the-shelf counter-fitted vector set (if obtainable offline) on the project's evaluation harness to get an external ceiling for the syn>ant metric.

### H. Hybrid designs: typed dimensions, per-POS subspaces, domain + scale

**How it works.** Treat the space as several typed blocks rather than one flat basis: (1) category/domain axes (what kind of thing: from lexicographer files, a depth-cut of the noun hierarchy, VerbNet classes for verbs), (2) scale axes (two-ended, signed: from adjective clusters, `attribute` links, possibly affect/sensorimotor scales), (3) operator axes (negation, degree, known/new, tense: closed-class words only, already built for function words, 614 axes with the seven extension axes), (4) optionally relational/role axes (GOAL, SOURCE). Similarity is then block-wise (e.g., domain match gate x scale agreement) or a weighted sum, and each block can have its own normalisation (e.g., IDF on categories, none on scales). Gärdenfors' domains/dimensions [V-s] and NSM's primes-plus-molecules layering are precedents for typed structure. Per-POS subspaces: adjectives on scales, nouns on categories+properties, verbs on classes+event structure, adverbs inherit from the adjective they derive from (derivational pointers; INFERENCE).

**Data.** Entirely WordNet-derivable for blocks (1) and (2) (lexname, hypernym ancestors, adjective clusters, attribute), VerbNet optional. All synset-id based, hence transferable to other wordnets (INFERENCE; subject to the lemma-level caveat on antonym pointers).

**Dimensions / coverage.** Category block ~44 (lex) to ~250 (depth-3 ancestors); scale block from ~hundreds (after merging) to ~2,000 (raw); operator block ~15-20. Plausibly 300-600 named axes in total, but with far less overlap (each sense uses few axes in each block), which is the intent: low effective dimension per word, not a small global axis count.

**Opposites etc.** Opposites: same category block, opposite signs in the scale block (happy/unhappy: same domain, opposite ends). Near-synonyms: same category, same side, close. Unrelated: different category block and/or different scales. Hypernyms: category block, with scale values inherited down. Polysemy: per sense. Non-gradable, converse, reversive opposites: still not covered by scales; remain relations (or get typed "complementary" scales with two values only; INFERENCE).

**Fit.** R1 partial (many axes, few active per word; "minimum" must be defined, O3); R2 pass; R3 pass (all derived from WordNet); R4 partial-pass; R5 partial (gradable adjectives, plus any signed scales found for nouns/verbs); R6 pass (operators typed and separate by construction, which also eases R6 concerns about conflating dimension types); R7 low-risk only if built additively behind a version flag.

**History.** The current space already mixes types in one block (primes, `attr:`, `lex:`), with cosine over all of them (history s0). M28.1 domain features helped; M20 showed the `lex:` block "not load-bearing as a block". Typed blocks with separate comparison rules were not tried; the owner's 2026-11-15 operator decisions already separate a fourth type.

**Risks.** Largest build; comparison rule becomes a design surface (two-stage, weights); the learned encoder/decoder and the memory model currently consume one flat vector (`sense_dense`), so the interface changes.

**Probe.** Take the five blocks as separate matrices over the same senses, measure each block's contribution on the scorer's examples (dog/cat, eat/chew, happy/unhappy/sad, unrelated) and on SimLex-999 by POS (block-wise cosine and a few simple combiners), without changing the artifact; report which combiner makes opposites and unrelated pairs behave. This costs reading the artifact, building scratch matrices, and no repo edit.

## 2b. Other traditions of semantic representation (second pass, 2026-11-15)

Same tags as above. Each tradition is described in the order: how meaning is stored; inventory size; named/interpretable?; per sense?; data and licence; multilingual; opposites / gradation / relatedness; fit (R1-R7); what the project could take. "Per sense" means the assignment is made to a word sense (synset-like), not to a word form.

### 2b.1 Sememe-based lexicons (HowNet, OpenHowNet) -- the closest existing analogue to "a minimal named inventory assigned per sense"

**How meaning is stored.** HowNet (Zhendong Dong and Qiang Dong; book: *HowNet and the Computation of Meaning*, World Scientific, 2006 [V]) defines each word sense by a definition written in sememes, "the smallest basic semantic units". Each sense is the root of a "sememe tree"; the relations between the sememes of one sense (called "dynamic roles") are the edges of that tree [V from the OpenHowNet paper]. Sememes themselves are organised in a taxonomy and fall into seven types: Thing, Part, Attribute, Time, Space, Attribute Value, Event [V]. Attributes and their values are organised in correspondence (e.g., for "paper": attribute colour with value white, attribute hardness with value soft [V-s, a CKIP report]). Each sememe is labelled in both Chinese and English [V].

**Inventory size (reported carefully; it varies by edition).** The OpenHowNet paper's table for the January 2019 release: 2,187 sememes; 229,767 senses; 127,266 distinct Chinese words; 104,025 distinct English words [V; Qi et al., arXiv:1901.09957, 2019; the PyPI record shows it as an arXiv preprint, I could not confirm a venue]. The OpenHowNet README (via search) says "about 2,000 sememes" and "over 200,000 senses" and a core data file of 237,973 concepts [V-s]. An early source reports 1,503 sememes [V-s, a 2002 source]; Dong's description "approximately 2,000" appears in a (retracted, see below) review [V-s]. So: roughly 1,500 (2002) -> 2,000-2,200 (2019). Average sememes per sense: not stated in the papers I read; I measured it from the data release: 2.42 (see 2b.1b). The data release lists 2,540 sememes, the 2019 paper's table 2,187.

**Named / per sense?** Named: yes, every sememe has an English and a Chinese label. Per sense: yes, every HowNet "concept" (sense) has its own definition; the same word form has several concepts with different definitions.

**Data and licence.** OpenHowNet is released by THUNLP with a Python API and, per the paper, "totally free" download requiring an email address [V]; an ecosystem index and a PyPI mirror list the repository licence as MIT [V-s; I did not read the LICENSE file, and whether the data carry the same terms is not confirmed]. The original, fuller HowNet is a product of HowNet Technology Inc. [V]; OpenHowNet is its "core data" released "with the support of the founders" [V].

**Multilingual.** Native bilingual (Chinese + English). Multilingual extension: BabelSememe (Qi, Chang, Sun, Ouyang, Liu, "Towards Building a Multilingual Sememe Knowledge Base: Predicting Sememes for BabelNet Synsets", AAAI 2020, pp. 8624-8631 [V]): 15,756 BabelNet synsets annotated with sememes (43,154 sememe annotations reported), inter-annotator Krippendorff's alpha 0.702, plus models for automatic prediction [V]. This is small compared with 117,659 WordNet synsets. How HowNet English entries map to WordNet synsets: not established from sources I read; BabelNet is the reported bridge [U for coverage].

**Sememe prediction and sememe-using embeddings.** Xie, Yuan, Liu, Sun, "Lexical Sememe Prediction via Word Embeddings and Matrix Factorization", IJCAI 2017, pp. 4200-4206 [V]: SPWE (collaborative-filtering style, from word-embedding neighbours) and SPSE (matrix factorisation of word-sememe relations), plus an ensemble. Niu, Xie, Liu, Sun, "Improved Word Representation Learning with Sememes", ACL 2017, pp. 2049-2058 [V]: three skip-gram-based models that learn sememe, sense and word embeddings with attention; gains on word similarity and analogy [V]. These are trained: they do not apply to a deterministic placement but show that sememe information is useful to embeddings and that the inventory can be extended by prediction. A review, "Sememe knowledge computation: a review of recent advances in application and expansion of sememe knowledge bases" (Frontiers of Computer Science), carries a retraction notice on the publisher page, so I did not rely on it [V-s].

**Opposites / gradation / relatedness.** (Superseded by 2b.1b, which measures this from the data: antonym and converse relations between sememes, a `neg` adjunct, a `degree` relation.) From the papers alone I could not verify how HowNet encodes antonymy, converse, or gradable adjectives [U]. One 2023 paper (ECML-PKDD, "Exploring Word-Sememe Graph-Centric Chinese Antonym Detection") detects Chinese antonyms from a word-sememe graph, which suggests antonymy is recoverable from sememe structure rather than being a given relation, but this is an inference from a title and snippet [U]. Relatedness: words with overlapping sememe trees are related; the hierarchy of sememes gives hypernym-like generalisation [V-s]. Per Dong's design (from memory, [U]), HowNet also stores explicit lexical relations (antonym, converse, entailment) in a separate relation file; check the primary documentation.

**Fit.**
- R1 partial: ~2,200 units, more than 607, but each sense uses a handful; "minimum" is a design aim of the authors, not a measured minimum [U].
- R2 pass: named, bilingual labels; no rotation.
- R3 partial: hand-curated by lexicographers, deterministic once given, no trained per-word values; the prediction models are learned (fail if used for placement).
- R4 partial: Chinese-English native; BabelSememe small.
- R5 unknown [U].
- R6 not examined: whether function-word-like sememes and operator-like sememes are separated from meaning sememes was not verified [U].
- R7 high interest: the only resource found that stores a closed named inventory per sense at scale.

**What the project could take.** (1) The shape: a closed, named inventory with each sense's meaning being a small tree of inventory items with typed relations between them: this matches the project's "trees nest" design and its sense = genus + differentia blend (M46), but with an explicit, closed, hand-checked inventory instead of 9,946 core words as the "axes". (2) The type system: Thing / Part / Attribute / Attribute Value / Event / Space / Time: attribute-value pairing is a data-level version of the project's `attr:` axes with signs (value poles of an attribute). (3) A cross-check: HowNet is an independent source for "how many named units, how many per sense, which units are ubiquitous", which tells the owner whether a minimal named inventory with few units per sense is achievable. (4) Evaluation: sememe prediction results give a published baseline for "predict a closed named inventory for a sense from text", relevant to what a deterministic mapping would have to match.

### 2b.1b HowNet / OpenHowNet in depth (MEASURED-NOW from the OpenHowNet data release; second pass)

Method. I downloaded the OpenHowNet resource archive named in the package's own download code (`resources.zip`, ~75 MB, from the THUNLP storage host) into a scratch directory, read the pickle files with a restricted unpickler that refuses every global (so only plain dicts/lists/strings load), and counted with scripts kept outside the repo. Sememe tokens were extracted with a regular expression over the DEF strings, so counts are approximate (+/- a few percent). Origin-language is treated as neutral (owner input, 2026-11-15); the questions that matter below are structure, coverage of the mapping to WordNet senses, and licence.

**Inventory and organisation.**
- Count: the data release lists **2,540 sememes** (the API says "There are 2540 sememes in HowNet" [V, README]; the file `sememe_all` holds 2,540 entries with a usage count each). The OpenHowNet paper's January 2019 table says 2,187 sememes [V]; the README prose says "about 2,000" [V]. The difference (2,187 vs 2,540) is not explained in the sources I read; I measured 2,192 distinct sememes actually appearing in DEF strings (so ~350 listed sememes are not used by any definition in this release, or my regex misses some).
- Taxonomy: a separate file lists sememe-sememe relations: **1,303 hypernym links, 241 antonym pairs, 114 converse pairs**; 1,741 sememes participate, 1,303 of them have a hypernym; maximum depth 7, typical depth 2-3 (counts: depth 1: 286; 2: 370; 3: 405; 4: 144; 5: 46; 6: 45; 7: 7). The taxonomy is shallow and incomplete (about 800 listed sememes have no entry at all in it). Top of the tree by number of descendants: Attribute (225), AttributeValue (178), entity (148), then event-like families (AlterLocation 90, AlterAttribute 74, AlterKnowledge 47, change 42, ShowEmotion 37, AlterPossession 35, event 30...). The paper names seven sememe types: Thing, Part, Attribute, Time, Space, Attribute Value, Event [V].
- Attribute / AttributeValue pairing is explicit: for example `SizeValue` has children `big` and `small`; `SpeedValue` has `fast` and `slow`; `FormValue` (under AppearanceValue under AttributeValue) has round, square, angular, netlike... [MEASURED-NOW; the FormValue hyponyms are also shown in the README [V]].
- Frequency profile: median sememe is used by 59 senses; 488 sememes by <=5 senses; 95 by >=1,000. The most frequent: human 22,115; China 20,418; character 14,679; part 14,178; ProperName 10,670; place 10,416; tool 6,877; fact 6,690; InstitutePlace 6,361; FuncWord 6,147; AnimalHuman 4,901; time 4,784. The single most frequent sememe is on 9.3% of all 237,974 senses (compare 98% for the project's SOMETHING axis). So HowNet has heavy-tailed but no near-universal sememes, partly because its definitions are specific (genus-like main sememe differs per sense), and partly because many generic notions are not encoded at all.

**What a sense's definition looks like (the DEF structure).** A data record has fields NO (id), W_C / W_E (Chinese and English word), G_C / G_E (part of speech), S_C / S_E (sentiment orientation, e.g., PlusFeeling), E_C / E_E (examples), DEF (the definition), RMK [V, README]. DEF is a tree: a **main (root) sememe**, optionally followed by `:` and relation-labelled children `relation={sememe...}`; `~` stands for the headword itself; `$` marks an open argument slot; sibling items in braces are separate children [V for the notation, from the README's examples]. Relation labels in use (counts of occurrences in DEF strings, MEASURED-NOW): modifier 42,098; domain 35,064; location 19,664; whole 17,977; agent 15,116; patient 14,057; instrument 13,863; content 13,096; host 12,206; PartPosition 10,807; RelateTo 10,145; scope 9,980; CoEvent 9,098; PlaceSect 8,972; manner 8,513; possession 6,085; TimeSect 3,672; belong 3,465; time 3,198; PatientProduct 3,138; cause 2,878; means 2,690; concerning 2,676; quantity 2,363; ResultEvent 2,350; degree 2,257; experiencer 1,951; material 1,905; purpose 1,803; and others. That is a closed role set of a few dozen (frame-element-like) relations, each a named edge, on top of the 2,540-sememe vocabulary.

**Real examples (OpenHowNet data, English-labelled entries; the Chinese word is the entry's key, the English word is its gloss).** Count of distinct English words per example sense list shown in parentheses.
1. **Concrete noun, dog:** `狗 (dog)` = `{livestock|牲畜:modifier={domesticated|家养}}`. **cat:** `猫` = `{livestock|牲畜:modifier={domesticated|家养},{recreation|娱乐:instrument={~}}}` (adds one pet-like facet). **sheep** `{livestock|牲畜}`, **horse** `{livestock|牲畜}`, **pig** `{livestock|牲畜}`: identical to each other. Wild animals: wolf `狼` = `{beast|走兽}`; lion `狮` = `{beast|走兽}`. So HowNet separates domesticated / wild but NOT species (sheep = horse = pig; wolf = lion): its resolution for animals is coarser than WordNet's.
2. **Concrete noun with structure, table / chair:** `台 (table)` = `{furniture|家具:{put|放置:LocationFin={~}}}`; `坐椅 (chair)` = `{furniture|家具:{sit|坐蹲:location={~}}}` (a function-based differentia: what is put/sat on it). **apple (fruit)** `{fruit|水果}`; the tree sense `{tree|树:{reproduce|生殖:PatientProduct={fruit|水果},agent={~}}}`; the company/computer sense: `{computer|电脑:modifier={PatternValue|样式值:CoEvent={able|能:scope={bring|携带:patient={$}}}}{SpeBrand|特定牌子}}` [V for the tree and computer examples, README; the rest MEASURED-NOW].
3. **Antonym pair, happy / unhappy:** `快乐 / 乐意 (happy)` = `{joyful|喜悦}`; `不愉快 / 不高兴 (unhappy)` = `{joyful|喜悦:adjunct={neg|否}}` -- unhappy is built as the same main sememe plus a negation adjunct. **sad:** `伤心` = `{sorrowful|悲哀}` (a different sememe, a child of `FeelingByBad|坏情`, while joyful is under `FeelingByGood|好情`).
4. **Antonym pair with separate sememes, hot / cold:** `热 (hot)` = `{hot|热}`, `冷 (cold)` = `{cold|冷}`, linked in the taxonomy file by an `antonym` relation between the two sememes. Likewise big `大` = `{big|大}` vs small `小` = `{small|小}`; fast `快` = `{fast|快}` vs slow `慢` = `{slow|慢}`; good `佳` = `{HighQuality|优质}` vs bad `劣` = `{inferior|劣质}` (also an `antonym` pair); alive `活着` = `{animate|有生命}` vs dead `死` = `{inanimate|无生命}`.
5. **Gradable adjective, degree and scale:** `太低 (too little)` = `{few|少:scope={Amount|多少:degree={very|很}}}`; tall `颀长` = `{StatureTall|身高}` vs short `短小` = `{StatureShort|身矮}` (stature-specific sememes) vs the generic `{high|高}` (tall, 桀). The `degree={very|很}` relation encodes intensification inside a definition; 2,257 DEF strings use a `degree` relation (about 1% of senses). Scalar position is mostly carried by distinct value sememes (big, small, SizeValue children), not by a numeric field.
6. **Verbs, eat / chew:** `吃 (eat)` = `{eat|吃:RelateTo={edible|食物}}` or `{eat|吃:patient={edible|食物}}`; `咀嚼 (chew)` = `{masticate|咀嚼}`. In the sememe taxonomy `eat` is under `metabolize|代谢` and `masticate` under `AlterForm|变形状`: so eat and chew share no sememe and no near ancestor in this release (their relatedness is not recoverable from the definition alone). A manner variant: `狼吞虎咽 (wolf down)` = `{eat|吃:manner={rash|莽}}`.
7. **Verbs, buy / sell:** `买 (buy)` = `{buy|买}`, `卖 / 出售 (sell)` = `{sell|卖}`; the taxonomy marks `buy` and `sell` as `converse` of each other, and places `buy` under `take|取` and `sell` under `give|给`. Other converse pairs in the file: agree / disagree, arrive / leave, begin / end, open / shut, study / teach, come / go, hide / reveal. Antonym pairs listed in the file include cheap / expensive, empty / full, dried / wet, StatureShort / StatureTall, formed / unformed, gentle / fierce, normal / abnormal.
8. **Function word, not:** `不 (not)` = `{FuncWord|功能词:adjunct={neg|否}}`. The same `neg|否` sememe that closed-class "not" uses is also used inside content words such as unhappy (466 adjective senses and 1,559 senses in total contain `neg`). This is the opposite of the owner's 2026-11-15 rule 3 (no negation value on content words); see the "kept and lost" subsection.

**Sememes per sense (MEASURED-NOW).** Across the 220,308 senses that carry an English word: mean **2.42** distinct sememes, median 2. Distribution (distinct sememes: senses): 1: 88,226; 2: 47,412; 3: 33,578; 4: 26,569; 5: 13,006; 6: 6,208; 7: 3,240; 8: 1,277; 9: 469; 10+: 323. By part of speech (mean): noun 3.09 (115,572 senses); verb 1.81 (57,243); adjective 1.51 (27,854); adverb 1.26 (8,738). For comparison the project's senses carry ~24 non-zero axes each (history s7).

**Resolution.** The 220,308 English-labelled senses collapse to only **35,119 distinct DEF strings** (about 6.3 senses per distinct definition); the most repeated DEF is `{FuncWord|功能词}` (3,272 senses), then Chinese county/city/district place names (each ~1,000-1,800). So a HowNet definition identifies a *class of senses*, not an individual one, much as one WordNet synset identifies a class of synonymous lemmas, but coarser: it cannot separate sheep from horse, or glad from happy (both `{joyful}`).

**How opposites and degree are encoded (summary).** Three devices, all explicit: (i) distinct sememes for the two poles, tied by a sememe-level `antonym` relation (241 pairs) or `converse` relation (114 pairs) in the taxonomy file; (ii) a negation adjunct (`neg|否`) on the same main sememe (unhappy = joyful + neg); (iii) a `degree` relation with intensifier sememes (`very|很`). MEASURED-NOW: 96,272 English-labelled senses (43.7%) contain at least one sememe that has an antonym or converse partner; among adjective senses, 21,162 of 27,854 (76%). Consequence for flat use: because poles are DIFFERENT sememes, a flat sememe-set similarity (Jaccard or cosine) gives hot/cold 0.0 and big/small 0.0 (disjoint sets) -- the opposite failure from the project's 0.85-0.89, while happy/unhappy gives 0.5 (shared `joyful`) and sad/unhappy gives 0 (`sorrowful` vs `joyful+neg`). Whether disjoint poles are treated as "far" or as "same dimension, opposite ends" depends on whether the consumer reads the `antonym` relation and the sememe taxonomy (SizeValue contains both big and small; hot/cold share an attribute parent): flat sets lose that.

**Mapping to WordNet / BabelNet and share of senses covered (MEASURED-NOW).**
- BabelSememe (Qi, Chang, Sun, Ouyang, Liu, AAAI 2020 [V]): the released data hold 15,755 BabelNet synsets (n 10,416; a 2,507; v 2,290; r 542), all with sememes, 14,341 with Chinese synonyms; mean 2.74 sememes per synset, median 2 [MEASURED-NOW; the paper says 15,756 synsets and 43,154 sememe annotations, inter-annotator alpha 0.702 [V]]. The sememe annotation of a BabelNet synset is a *flat list of sememes* (the `sememes` field is a list of strings), not a DEF tree.
- To WordNet 3.0 (117,659 synsets): matching each WordNet synset to a BabelSememe synset by same part of speech and Jaccard overlap of lemma sets >= 0.5 gives **12,022 synsets (10.2%)** (verbs 4,323 of 13,767; adjectives 3,994 of 18,156; nouns 3,008 of 82,115; adverbs 697 of 3,621); with identical lemma sets: 7,832 (6.7%). Matching by exact gloss text found only 1,068 (the glosses differ). So the *sense-level* mapping to WordNet is ~10% via BabelSememe, concentrated on verbs and adjectives (the annotators chose frequent items), and very thin on nouns (3.7%).
- Word-level (not sense-level) overlap: 121,342 distinct English word types in HowNet; 53,703 of WordNet's 147,306 lemma types (36.5%) occur among them (many WordNet lemmas are proper names or multi-word); 55.7% of WordNet synsets (65,511 of 117,659) have at least one lemma that is a HowNet English word (nouns 39,256, verbs 12,901, adjectives 11,073, adverbs 2,281). But HowNet's English senses are glosses of Chinese entries (a given English word sits under several Chinese words, mean 2.4 HowNet senses per shared word vs 2.1 WordNet senses), so word-level overlap has to be turned into sense-level overlap by disambiguation (a WSD step the project has found hard, M30).
- Therefore the **mapping question is open**: the released BabelSememe is the only sense-identifier bridge I found, and it covers about a tenth of WordNet. A deterministic route (map each WordNet synset to a HowNet sense via gloss/lemma overlap and the DEF's main sememe) would have to be built and evaluated; sememe prediction (SPWE/SPSE, BabelNet prediction models) is trained and would break R3 if used for placement.

**Licence and redistribution.** The OpenHowNet repository is listed as MIT-licensed (README badge; ecosystem index) [V-s, I did not read a LICENSE file]; the paper says the data download is "totally free" with an email address for notifications [V]; HowNet itself is a product of HowNet Technology Inc. and OpenHowNet is "the core data" released with the founders' support [V]; the licence of the *data* inside the archive (as opposed to the Python code) is not stated in the README text I read [U]; BabelNet-derived content (glosses, ids) in `babel_data` is subject to BabelNet's own licence, which I did not verify [U]. Derived resources (BabelSememe, sememe-prediction outputs, sememe embeddings) are published by THUNLP on GitHub with their own repositories [V-s].

**Comparison with the NSM prime set (size and spirit).** Sizes: NSM 65 primes (plus a few hundred "semantic molecules" in later work [V-s]) vs HowNet 2,187-2,540 sememes. Spirit: NSM primes are undefinable, cross-linguistically lexicalised words and are meant to define EVERY other word in explications written in ordinary sentences (a "reductive paraphrase"); HowNet sememes are a larger lexicographer-chosen vocabulary of "basic units", and a sense is a *tree* with typed relations, many of whose sememes are themselves defined by taxonomy (hypernym links), i.e., HowNet's inventory is partly derived from its own hierarchy and is not claimed to be irreducible. I looked for published work that maps or compares sememes and NSM primes and did not find any [U]; the closest were papers describing HowNet as inspired by the same Leibniz-style idea ("some linguists believe that the meanings of all words in any language can be represented by a limited set of sememes" [V, README]) without citing NSM. Where the two obviously touch: HowNet has `neg`, `very`, `many`/`few`, `big`/`small`, `good`-like value sememes, `1stPerson|我`, `2ndPerson|你`, `3rdPerson|他` (MEASURED-NOW in the inventory), i.e., many prime-like items exist as sememes too.

**Published evaluations of sememe-based vectors (what I could verify).**
- Niu et al., ACL 2017 [V]: sememe-encoded skip-gram models (SAT best on analogy) improve word similarity and analogy over skip-gram/CBOW. The repository's updated table reports analogy overall 84.5 for SAT vs 73.4 Skip-gram and 64.2 CBOW, and on Chinese similarity sets SAT leads on WordSim-240 (61.2) and ties on WordSim-297 (63.3 vs 63.3) [V-s, repository README via search; the authors note the new numbers may differ from the paper's tables]. These are *Chinese* embedding results and trained.
- Xie et al., IJCAI 2017 [V]: sememe *prediction* from embeddings (SPWE, SPSE and an ensemble); accuracy numbers not read [U].
- OpenHowNet itself ships a similarity calculation via sememes (README: "calculate word similarity via sememes") [V]; its quality on English benchmarks (SimLex-999, antonym sets) I could not find [U]; no evaluation on antonym vs synonym discrimination of sememe-based vectors was found [U]. A 2023 Chinese antonym detection paper (word-sememe graph) exists [V-s: title/snippet only].

**Known criticisms and limits (documented vs inferred).**
- Hand-annotated over ~30 years by two people, per the README ("spent almost 30 years building HowNet") [V]; consistency across annotators for the original HowNet is not reported in the sources I read [U]; BabelSememe's own annotation agreement is alpha 0.702 [V].
- Resolution: 35,119 distinct definitions for 220,308 English senses (measured); species-level distinctions missing for animals; eat and chew unrelated; opposites split across different sememes. Granularity differs from WordNet's.
- Coverage: sense-level English mapping to WordNet ~10% via the released BabelSememe; word-level ~56% of synsets share a lemma; the English side is a gloss layer on Chinese entries (many English strings are inflected forms or proper names: aardwolves, abaci, Frenchmen [V, README]).
- Taxonomy incompleteness: 1,741 of 2,540 sememes appear in the taxonomy file; only 1,303 have a hypernym.
- Flat vs structured use: most published sememe-using models flatten a sense to its sememe set (e.g., Niu et al.'s models use sememe sets with attention [V]); BabelSememe annotations are flat sets [MEASURED-NOW]; the relation-labelled tree structure (agent, patient, modifier...) is the part that flattening throws away. Whether trees can be flattened into a fixed vector without loss is the same question the owner decided on 2026-11-15 for the project's own trees (node-by-node or composed come out near-equivalent once flattened).
- Opposition is partial: 19% of sememes (482 of 2,540) are in an antonym/converse relation; other contrasts exist only as distinct, unrelated sememes.
- Negation on content words (neg inside unhappy) mixes operator and meaning dimensions (conflicts with owner rule 3, R6).

**What would be KEPT and what LOST if sememes replaced the prime axes for content words while NSM primes stayed for closed-class and operator words (stated plainly; not a recommendation).**
- *Kept:* all closed-class and operator dimensions (the 7 extension axes, the NOT/VERY/MORE/KNOWN... family, the per-language lexicon), because they would still be filled by primes and lexicon entries; the tree contract; the scorer's folding of operators into skeletons; synset-id multilinguality if the mapping can be built; deterministic placement.
- *Lost:* (a) the 65-prime floor as the definitional vocabulary for content-word senses (M46 grounding, OOV fallback); (b) WordNet-derived signals now built into the 607 axes (`attr:` axes from the `attribute` relation, the 44 `lex:` categories), unless re-derived; (c) the property that every axis is on a known propagation graph (WordNet synonym/similar/also_see edges): sememe values come from hand annotation (HowNet) rather than propagation, so senses outside the mapped ~10% would have NO coordinates until the mapping or a deterministic derivation exists; (d) comparability with the frozen artifact and its fingerprint, the trained chooser at d=607 and the v6 encoder; (e) fine noun distinctions (sheep vs horse) unless extra sememes are added.
- *Changed:* the ubiquitous-axis problem disappears at the top (max sememe frequency 9.3%) but a new low-frequency / zero-overlap problem appears (hot/cold disjoint, eat/chew disjoint); opposites become "different sememe + antonym relation" rather than "nearly identical vector"; the content-word axis count rises from 607 to about 2,200-2,500, with ~2.4 active per sense (vs ~24 now).
- *What would have to be true for it to work:* (1) a deterministic mapping from WordNet (and other-language) senses onto HowNet senses covering most of the project's 117,659 senses (today ~10% via BabelSememe), or a deterministic rule that derives sememe-style definitions (main sememe + relation-linked modifiers) for the rest from WordNet relations; (2) the tree is either kept (relation-labelled children as additional named coordinates, e.g., sememe x relation pairs) or the flat set is shown to suffice on the project's pair tasks; (3) the antonym/converse relations and the sememe taxonomy are used at comparison time (or folded into coordinates) so that poles are not simply disjoint; (4) negation-bearing definitions (neg inside unhappy) are re-expressed without an operator sememe, or the owner relaxes rule 3 for lexicalised negatives; (5) the licence permits shipping derived coordinates; (6) held-out evaluation on SimLex/antonym/hypernym sets shows an improvement over the current space, since no published English evaluation exists.

### 2b.2 Primitive-decomposition traditions

**Componential analysis / Katz & Fodor (1963).** Meaning of a word = set of semantic markers (hypothesised atomic concepts) plus a residue called distinguishers; markers are arranged in tree-structured "readings" per sense (Katz & Fodor, "The Structure of a Semantic Theory", *Language* 39(2):170-210 [V]). Binary features such as +/-ANIMATE, +/-MALE, +/-ADULT are the textbook form; opposites in closed sets come out as one feature flipped (man/woman, boy/girl). Inventory: open and undecided; I did not find a fixed size [U]. Named, per sense: yes. Opposites: complementaries/kinship/gender work as a sign flip on one feature (this is exactly the owner's intuition); gradables are not binary. Relatedness: shared features. Recurring problem inside the theory itself: the distinguisher residue (what is not decomposed) was never systematically defined.

**Schank's Conceptual Dependency (CD).** Events are represented with a small set of primitive acts, e.g., PTRANS (physical transfer), ATRANS (abstract transfer, e.g., possession), MTRANS (mental transfer of information) [V-s; Schank et al. 1972 Stanford AI memo AIM-162 on DTIC, and course handouts]. Reported number of primitive acts varies by source (11, 12, "14 or so") [V-s]. Language-independent by intent. Verbs map to acts + roles (e.g., give/buy/steal share ATRANS with different participants), which handles converses (buy/sell) as the same act with the roles swapped, a feature the project lacks. Nouns/adjectives are outside CD's core. Hand-built, small, very coverage-limited.

**Wilks' preference semantics.** Word senses carry "formulae" built from a few dozen semantic primitives, and sentence meaning is found by maximising semantic density/coherence using preferences (Wilks, "A preferential, pattern-seeking, semantics for natural language inference", *Artificial Intelligence* 6(1):53-74, 1975 [V]). I did not retrieve the formulas or the primitive inventory size [U]. Interest: preferences are selectional constraints, i.e., what a sense expects of its neighbours.

**Jackendoff's Conceptual Semantics.** A finite set of conceptual primitives and combination principles; functions like GO, BE, STAY, CAUSE, INCH apply across fields (spatial, possession, property ascription, scheduling) with the field, not the primitive, distinguishing uses (Jackendoff, *Semantic Structures*, MIT Press, 1990 [V-s via secondary descriptions; GO/BE/STAY/INCH/CAUSE list not checked against the book]). The attractive idea for this project: one small set of event-structure primitives reused across domains. The standard criticism: the primitives are very abstract and hard to justify empirically [V-s].

**Pustejovsky's Generative Lexicon** is covered in 2b/F (four qualia roles: formal, constitutive, telic, agentive [V-s]).

**Leibniz -> Wierzbicka -> NSM.** Wierzbicka traces NSM to Leibniz's hypothetical "alphabet of human thoughts", revived in the 1960s by Andrzej Boguslawski; her 1972 *Semantic Primitives* proposed 14 elements; the set grew to 60 (2002) and 65 [V-s, from Wierzbicka's 2021 retrospective, "'Semantic Primitives', fifty years later", *Russian Journal of Linguistics* 25(2)]. NSM differs from CD/Katz-Fodor in insisting that primes are words of every natural language (found by cross-linguistic study) and in using semantic molecules (non-primitive building blocks such as "hands", "water") [V-s].

**Criticism and evidence.** Fodor, Garrett, Walker and Parkes, "Against definitions", *Cognition* 8:263-367 (1980) [V]: reviews arguments for definitions (reference, validity, comprehension, learning), judges non-definitional accounts preferable, and reports comprehension experiments on causative verbs; widely seen as damaging to decompositional theories [V-s]. Earlier: Fodor's argument that "kill" and "cause to die" are not truth-equivalent in all contexts [V-s]; Fodor, Fodor and Garrett (1975), "The psychological unreality of semantic representations", negation experiments (e.g., bachelor vs unmarried) [V-s]. Defences: Pitt (1999), "In defense of definitions", *Philosophical Psychology* 12(2) argues the critique misrepresents decomposition and its tests are flawed [V-s]; Stringer (2012) argues child acquisition of locative verbs shows lexical decomposition [V-s]. Net reading (INFERENCE): the evidence against decomposition mostly targets necessary-and-sufficient definitions and processing-time claims; it does not rule out graded, named feature spaces. The project's space is graded and defines words by position, not by definition, so the critique bites less on it but bites hard on any claim that the inventory is the true set of primitives.

**Fit (the family).** R1 partial (small sets are the aim; no agreement on which); R2 pass; R3 fail/partial (hand-assigned features; the data-derived exception is a feature read from a dictionary relation); R4 partial (NSM explicitly cross-linguistic; CD language-independent by intent); R5 pass for binary/complementary contrasts, fail for gradables; R6 pass; R7: long record of coverage failure outside hand-built domains.

**What the project could take.** (1) Binary features for CLOSED contrast sets (gender, number, animacy, kinship, perhaps possession) where a sign flip is faithful; (2) CD-style event primitives (transfer of physical/abstract/mental) as candidate verb axes, which handles buy/sell (same act, swapped roles) in a way scale axes cannot; (3) Jackendoff's reuse of one primitive across fields as a design test: does one named axis serve space, possession and state?; (4) the lesson of the Fodor critique: do not claim the inventory is the truth; claim it is a working named coordinate system.

### 2b.4 Vector-symbolic / hyperdimensional storage (the project's tree container)

**How it works.** Roles and fillers are vectors; binding combines a role vector with a filler vector; a structure is the superposition (sum) of its bindings; unbinding with a role retrieves a noisy filler that is cleaned up against a memory of known fillers. Tensor product representations (Smolensky, "Tensor product variable binding and the representation of symbolic structures in connectionist systems", *Artificial Intelligence* 46(1-2):159-216, 1990 [V]) use the outer product (dimension grows as role dimension x filler dimension). Holographic reduced representations (Plate, *IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks* 6(3):623-641, 1995 [V]) use circular convolution to keep a fixed dimension; capacity analysis is in the paper (a secondary summary says capacity grows roughly linearly with dimension [V-s]). Kanerva's "Hyperdimensional Computing: An Introduction...", *Cognitive Computation* 1:139-159, 2009 [V] and Gayler's "Vector Symbolic Architectures answer Jackendoff's challenges for cognitive neuroscience" (ICCS 2003, arXiv cs/0412059 [V]) argue the family answers Jackendoff's four challenges for connectionism (binding, variables, etc.). Eliasmith et al., "A Large-Scale Model of the Functioning Brain", *Science* 338(6111):1202-1205, 2012 [V] (Spaun, "Semantic Pointer Architecture Unified Network", 2.5 million simulated neurons) uses semantic pointers (compressed vectors that bind to structures); Eliasmith's 2013 book *How to Build a Brain* [U]. Survey: Kleyko, Rachkovskij, Osipov, Rahimi, "A Survey on Hyperdimensional Computing aka Vector Symbolic Architectures, Part I", *ACM Computing Surveys* 55(6):130, 2022 [V]; it lists TPR, HRR, Multiply-Add-Permute, Binary Spatter Codes and Sparse Binary Distributed Representations as models [V].

**What they say about the filler (word-meaning) space.**
- The filler vectors may be arbitrary. In most VSA models fillers are random high-dimensional vectors (similarity between distinct atoms ~0), which makes similarity between structures reflect structure only; TPR allows any filler vectors, including interpretable ones [V-s; INFERENCE for the interpretable case, consistent with the project's use].
- Graded similarity: superposition and binding preserve similarity in the way that the similarity of two bound structures grows with the similarity of their role-matched fillers (INFERENCE from the construction). So a graded, interpretable filler space makes tree comparison graded, which is what the node-by-node and composed tree scorers rely on (owner decision 4, 2026-11-15).
- Dimension cost: with TPR the structure dimension is roles x fillers, so the number of USVS axes multiplies into memory size (INFERENCE); with convolution-based binding the filler dimension is fixed and a 600-2,000 axis named space must be projected or used as-is with a fixed dimension, at some capacity cost. This is a practical, non-semantic reason to prefer fewer or sparser filler axes.
- Cleanup memory: retrieval requires a list of known fillers; with named axes, cleanup is nearest-neighbour over senses (works) but ubiquitous axes make nearest-neighbour noisy (the same issue as in tree scoring).
- None of the VSA literature I read constrains how the filler space should be defined semantically [V-s; INFERENCE from absence].

**Fit.** R1-R7 not applicable as a meaning scheme; compatible with every family. **What to take:** treat filler dimension and sparsity as a design cost (probe P14); keep fillers named, and use the VSA literature only for binding and capacity.

### 2b.6 Verb meaning specifically

**What the literature offers.**
- **Aspectual classes** (Vendler, "Verbs and times", *Philosophical Review* 66(2):143-160, 1957 [V]): states, activities, accomplishments, achievements (plus semelfactive, added later [V-s]). Four to five values, applies to predicates rather than bare verbs [V-s]; a typed dimension (an attribute with a small set of values), not a scale.
- **Thematic roles and proto-roles** (Dowty, "Thematic proto-roles and argument selection", *Language* 67(3):547-619, 1991 [V-s]): the number of discrete roles is unsettled; Dowty replaces them with graded proto-Agent/proto-Patient entailment bundles [V-s]. Relevant: verbs' participant structure is better as graded features than as a fixed role list.
- **FrameNet frames** (above); **VerbNet classes** (Levin-based, with roles and mappings to WordNet and FrameNet [V-s]); **Levin classes** (47 + 193 per a course deck [V-s]).
- **Manner/result** (Rappaport Hovav & Levin, "Reflections on Manner/Result Complementarity", in *Lexical Semantics, Syntax, and Event Structure*, OUP, 2010, pp. 21-38 [V-s]): a verb lexicalises manner or result, not both; result = scalar change, manner = non-scalar change [V-s]; contested by Beavers & Koontz-Garboden and by Goldberg [V-s]. Levin & Rappaport Hovav, *Argument Realization*, Cambridge University Press, 2005 [V-s]: how verb meaning determines argument expression.
- **WordNet's own verb organisation** (Fellbaum, "English Verbs as a Semantic Net", *International Journal of Lexicography* 3(4):278-301, 1990 [V-s]): troponymy ("is a manner of") instead of hypernymy; four kinds of lexical entailment [V-s]; later work says troponymy is polysemous: manner, function, result sub-relations [V-s].
- **Event knowledge resources:** ATOMIC (Sap et al., AAAI 2019, pp. 3027-3035 [V]): ~877,000 textual if-then descriptions, nine relation types (causes vs effects, agents vs themes, voluntary vs involuntary, actions vs mental states) [V]; ConceptNet event relations (covered).
- **Crowd-sourced verb features:** VerbCorner (Hartshorne, Bonial, Palmer, "The VerbCorner Project: Findings from Phase 1 of Crowd-Sourcing a Semantic Decomposition of Verbs", ACL 2014, pp. 397-402 [V]): crowd judgements about whether over a hundred commonly discussed semantic features hold for verbs in syntactic frames; tests that verbs with the same syntactic behaviour share semantic features; high inter-annotator agreement reported in a secondary review [V-s]. This is the nearest thing to a named, human-rated, per-verb-sense interpretable feature set for verbs. Binder's 65 attributes applied to verbs: not verified [U].

**WordNet verb facts (MEASURED-NOW, WordNet 3.0 via NLTK).** 13,767 verb synsets; 13,208 have a hypernym (the troponym hierarchy; mean maximal depth 2.5; 559 root-level synsets); 996 carry an antonym pointer; 390 have an entailment pointer; 218 a cause pointer; 1,498 belong to a verb group; all carry subcategorisation frames. 15 verb lexicographer classes: change 2,383; contact 2,196; communication 1,548; motion 1,408; social 1,106; possession 847; stative 756; cognition 695; creation 694; body 547; perception 461; competition 459; emotion 343; consumption 243; weather 81. Verb antonym pointers are mostly reversive or complementary pairs (examples: exhale/inhale, sleep/wake, fall_asleep/wake_up, go_to_bed/get_up, rest/be_active), not two ends of a scale. History: v-v had only 5 antonym pairs in the held-out test (history s1, M28.0).

**Why verbs are the weak spot for scale and taxonomy axes.** Taxonomy depth is low (2.5), so Wu-Palmer or ancestor sets separate verbs poorly; scale axes need adjective-like gradable properties, which most verbs lack; meaning of verbs lives in event structure (change of state, manner vs result, causation, transfer), participant structure and aspectual type.

**Candidate verb dimensions drawn from the literature (each derivable from data).** 15 lexicographer classes (data; already in `lex:`); aspectual type (Vendler; not in WordNet, derivable from VerbNet/FrameNet or from frames [U]); result-vs-manner (scalar change; from VerbNet class or Levin class [U]); causation and entailment (WordNet pointers: 218 and 390 verbs, thin); transfer type (CD's PTRANS/ATRANS/MTRANS, from VerbNet classes such as give/send/tell [INFERENCE]); participant proto-role bundle (VerbNet roles or VerbCorner judgments). Verb opposition as a typed relation: reversive/complementary/converse pairs stay relations (O6).

**Fit.** R1 partial; R2 pass; R3 pass for WordNet pointers and VerbNet/Levin classes (data), partial for VerbCorner (human-rated); R4 partial (VerbNet English; WordNet ids); R5 fail for verbs (reversive/complementary); R6 pass.

**What to take.** (1) A verb block of typed, few-valued dimensions (lexname + class + aspectual + result/manner) next to the existing scale-and-category content; (2) accept that verb opposition is relational; (3) VerbCorner and VerbNet as external gold for whether "eat" and "chew" share the features the literature says they should.

### 2b.7 Graph-derived minimal bases, labelled afterwards (owner input, 2026-11-15)

**Owner reading.** "At the end of the day it's a graph theory problem, but we could realistically have an LLM tag each dimension once we have the minimum number of dimensions." Requirement 2 (named, interpretable axes) may then be met AFTER the fact: dimensions are DERIVED from the lexical relation graph (the minimum set that reproduces its structure) and labelled afterwards. This re-opens approaches that I marked "fail: unnamed" in Family G and in 2b.4/2b.5 (spectral, non-negative and sparse decompositions). The rest of this subsection re-assesses them under that reading. In the comparison table they are row M.

**Where the line between "deterministic" and "trained per-word values" can be drawn (for the owner to confirm).** What the project rejected (M21b, M25, history s2.3) was optimising free per-word values with a loss, where more training made antonym separation worse (0.60 -> 0.54). A decomposition of a FIXED graph also produces a value per word, but the values are a function of the graph: same graph + same algorithm + fixed tie-breaks gives the same numbers. Three tiers:
- *Closed-form or convex, fixed matrix* (eigendecomposition of a Laplacian, SVD, greedy set cover with a fixed order, concept-lattice construction): reproducible up to well-defined ambiguities (eigenvector sign; arbitrary rotation inside a degenerate eigenspace; the measured eigenvalues in 2b.7's probe are nearly degenerate, so individual eigenvectors are NOT individually stable and need canonicalisation or must be grouped by eigenspace).
- *Non-convex but deterministic given a fixed initialisation* (NMF with a deterministic SVD-based initialisation and fixed update order): reproducible but a local optimum; uniqueness holds only under conditions on the data (Donoho & Stodden 2003 [V]).
- *Stochastic training* (autoencoders, SPINE, gradient descent with random seeds): reproducible only with a seed and not unique; this is the side the project's rule forbids.
The project's own record uses tier 1: the M19 dimensionality spectrum was an SVD of the word-by-axis matrix (history s6).

**Methods, how many dimensions they need, nameability, behaviour of opposites.**
1. **Laplacian eigenmaps / spectral embedding** (Belkin & Niyogi, *Neural Computation* 15(6):1373-1396, 2003 [V]): coordinates are the smallest eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian of a neighbourhood graph; locality-preserving. Number of dimensions: in spectral clustering the eigengap heuristic chooses the number of clusters (von Luxburg, "A tutorial on spectral clustering", *Statistics and Computing*, 2007 [V; the heuristic works when clusters are well separated and is ambiguous otherwise]). Nameability: eigenvectors are dense, signed, and sign/rotation-ambiguous; they become nameable when they align with clusters (indicator-like vectors). I found no verified study of spectral embeddings of WordNet's full relation graph for interpretability [U].
2. **Signed graphs, balance and the signed Laplacian** (Cartwright & Harary, "Structural balance: a generalization of Heider's theory", *Psychological Review* 63(5):277-293, 1956 [V]: a signed graph is balanced iff its vertices split into two sets with positive edges inside and negative edges across; Kunegis, Schmidt, Lommatzsch, Lerner, De Luca, Albayrak, "Spectral Analysis of Signed Graphs for Clustering, Prediction and Visualization", SDM 2010, pp. 559-570 [V]: signed Laplacian, signed spectral clustering and visualisation). Directly relevant to words: Sedoc, Gallier, Foster, Ungar, "Semantic Word Clusters Using Signed Spectral Clustering", ACL 2017 (arXiv 1601.05403) [V from the Anthology listing and abstract; I did not read experiments]: synonyms are positive edges, antonyms negative edges, and the authors report that clustering with signed cuts separates antonyms that plain embeddings place together. WordNet's adjective "dumbbell" (two satellite groups joined by an antonym pair, Gross & Miller 1990 [V-s]) is exactly a balanced two-camp signed subgraph; one eigenvector per balanced component carries "which camp".
3. **Non-negative matrix factorisation and sparse coding** (Lee & Seung, "Learning the parts of objects by non-negative matrix factorization", *Nature* 401, 1999 [V]: parts-based, additive; Donoho & Stodden, NIPS 2003, pp. 1141-1148 [V]: uniqueness when the data populate the faces of the orthant; Murphy, Talukdar, Mitchell NNSE, COLING 2012 [V]: sparse non-negative embeddings reported effective and interpretable). Number of dimensions: chosen by reconstruction error / a knee; no theory for "how few". Nameability: the best-evidenced of the group (parts are additive, non-negative, sparse; interpretability is assessed by intrusion tests, Chang et al. 2009 [V]; SEMCAT categories give an automatic proxy, Senel et al. 2018 [V]). Opposites: NMF is unsigned, so antonymy must be carried by a separate negative-edge term or by different components, exactly as with the current space.
4. **Basis selection / minimum description formulations.** *Metric dimension / resolving sets:* a minimum set of landmark nodes whose distance profile identifies every node; NP-hard in general with an O(log n) greedy approximation (Khuller, Raghavachari, Rosenfeld, "Landmarks in graphs", *Discrete Applied Mathematics* 70(3):217-229, 1996 [V-s]). *Test cover:* the minimum set of tests (features) that separates all items; Moret & Shapiro, "On minimizing a set of tests", *SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput.* 6, 1985 [V-s: existence and subject only]; NP-hard, solved greedily as set cover [V-s]. *Boolean matrix factorisation / the Discrete Basis Problem:* Miettinen, Mielikainen, Gionis, Das, Mannila, *IEEE TKDE* 20(10):1348-1362, 2008 [V]: NP-hard, greedy algorithm, bases are intuitive but reconstruction error is larger than with real-valued methods [V]. *Formal concept analysis:* Ganter & Wille, *Formal Concept Analysis: Mathematical Foundations*, Springer 1999 [V]: the concept lattice of a binary object-attribute context is unique and deterministic; each formal concept is a named intent (a set of attributes) and extent (objects); size of the lattice can be very large [U for a bound]. Number of dimensions: these give the answer to "how few" directly for a chosen separation requirement (the size of the cover) but only up to NP-hard/approximation. Nameability: Boolean and FCA bases are made of whole attributes, so they are as nameable as the attributes they are built from (e.g., 2,540 sememes or 607 axes).
5. **Communities / clusters as categories.** Modularity or spectral clusters of the relation graph give a partition (categories), not scales; a signed method gives bipolar clusters (camps) which are the dumbbells. No verified reference for a modularity-based category system over WordNet [U].

**Measured probes run for this report (read-only; scratch scripts; WordNet 3.0 and the shipped artifact).**
- *Adjective relation graph.* 18,156 adjective synsets; 12,130 positive edges (similar_to, also_see); 1,999 negative edges (head-to-head antonym pointers). Connected components over all edges: **4,809** (3,615 singletons; giant component 6,807 nodes; 14,541 nodes in components of size >= 2). A spectral basis spends (at least) one near-zero eigenvector per component, so the adjective graph alone has thousands of near-independent parts. Hence "the minimum dimension count from the graph" is NOT small unless components are tied together by other relations (attribute, derivation, hypernym of attribute nouns, category membership) or only the largest components are embedded.
- *Signed spectral embedding with held-out edges* (signed Laplacian L = D - A, smallest eigenvectors, cosine in the embedded space; 20% of antonym and similar edges held out before building; evaluation only inside the giant component of the training graph, 4,741 nodes; 170 held-out similar pairs and 72 held-out antonym pairs): held-out antonym pairs get **negative** cosine (-0.67 at k = 1, -0.85 at k = 5, -0.84 at k = 10, -0.80 at k = 20, -0.67 at k = 50, -0.54 at k = 100, -0.36 at k = 200, -0.06 at k = 400) while held-out similar pairs stay positive (0.84, 0.83, 0.79, 0.77, 0.64, 0.56, 0.31, 0.08); AUC of similar > antonym: 0.78 (k = 1), 0.92 (k = 2), **0.96 (k = 5)**, 0.96 (k = 10), 0.95 (k = 20), 0.94 (k = 50), 0.93 (k = 100), 0.87 (k = 200), 0.66 (k = 400). The smallest eigenvalues are nearly degenerate (0.0009, 0.0032, 0.0034, 0.0036, 0.0040...; gaps <= 0.002), so there is no eigengap telling k. Caveats: adjectives only; head-to-head antonym pairs; a link-prediction style test on a part of the graph, not the project's SimLex or antonym benchmarks; small samples (72 held-out antonym pairs). Reading: signed structure makes opposites opposite in a handful of dimensions, as balance theory predicts; low k is better than high k, and the signal vanishes at k = 400.
- *NMF of the shipped sense-by-axis matrix* (117,679 senses x 607 axes, scikit-learn NMF with nndsvda initialisation, 60 iterations, seed 0; cosine over 300,000 random pairs; the original space has mean random-pair cosine 0.398): k = 20: relative reconstruction error 0.487, correlation of pair cosines with the original 0.767, mean random cosine 0.343, 13.4 non-zero components per sense; k = 50: 0.286 / 0.750 / 0.241 / 24.9; k = 100: 0.169 / 0.732 / 0.180 / 35.4 (k = 200 was still running when this was written). So reproducing the PAIRWISE structure with a non-negative basis keeps a correlation of ~0.75 whether the basis has 20 or 100 components; the reconstruction error falls with k but the cosine correlation does not rise, consistent with the original cosines being dominated by the generic mass that a parts-based basis spreads across components. NMF components are not a minimum basis for this matrix: error is still 0.17 at 100 components.
- *Nameability of the NMF components (top-loading axes, k = 20).* Several components are recognisable: noun.person + SOMEONE + LIVE; noun.plant; noun.animal + SMALL + attr:biology; noun.artifact; noun.communication + WORDS + verb.communication + attr:law; noun.body + PART + noun.process + noun.shape; adj.all + KIND + PEOPLE; verb.change + noun.event + verb.stative + verb.possession. Every component also carries generic prime axes (SOMETHING, KIND, WHEN, ONE, PEOPLE), because the input matrix does: the labelling would be "category + generic mass". At k = 50 components mix domain tags (colloquialism, military, chess, boat) with lexicographer categories. So under this input the components are as nameable as the project's own `lex:`/`attr:` axes already are, no more: re-deriving from a derived matrix mostly reproduces its categories.

**Using the sememe graph instead.** The same methods apply to the sememe-by-sense incidence matrix (2,540 named sememes x ~220,000 senses) or to the sememe taxonomy graph with its 241 antonym and 114 converse relations as negative edges: NMF, Boolean factorisation or concept-lattice construction would compress 2,540 named units to fewer derived units (to be labelled afterwards) while keeping sparse, additive, non-negative senses. This is a probe (P17-P20), not a measured result.

**Labelling after the fact: what is known.**
- *Automated labelling by a language model.* Bills et al. (OpenAI, 2023), "Language models can explain neurons in language models" [V-s]: GPT-4 writes short explanations for GPT-2 neurons that another model scores by predicting activations; the large majority of explanations score poorly, about a thousand score >= 0.8, and GPT-4's explanations are worse than humans'. Anthropic's "Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning" (2023) [V-s: authors not confirmed in my searches]: a sparse autoencoder splits a 512-neuron layer into more than 4,000 features, auto-labelled by an LLM and scored by how well another model predicts the feature's activations. Huang, Geiger, D'Oosterlinck, Wu, Potts, "Rigorously Assessing Natural Language Explanations of Neurons", BlackboxNLP 2023, pp. 317-331 [V]: even the most confident GPT-4 explanations have high error rates and little to no causal efficacy; the authors question natural language as the explanation format and neurons as the unit.
- *Human validation formats.* Word intrusion and topic intrusion (Chang, Gerrish, Wang, Boyd-Graber, Blei, NIPS 2009 [V]): show the top words of a component plus one intruder; coherent components let people find the intruder. Category-overlap scoring (Senel et al. 2018, SEMCAT, 110 categories, >6,500 words [V]). Both fit this project: the "activating examples" of a derived dimension are WORD SENSES with known lexical relations, not text, so the labelling task is closer to topic-model labelling than to neuron explanation (INFERENCE).
- *Practical protocol (my suggestion, not a recommendation of the approach):* for each derived dimension give the labeller the top-loading senses, a random set of mid-loading senses, and negative examples; require a short label plus a one-line scope; validate by (i) intrusion tests on held-out senses, (ii) held-out membership prediction from the label alone, (iii) agreement across two independent labelling runs, (iv) a small human audit; record the label, the validation scores and the artifact fingerprint together.
- *The rule that would still hold:* **the labelling is documentation; the definition is the deterministic construction.** Labels are never inputs to placement or to any rule (that would reintroduce hand-written language patterns); a rebuild with a different graph or k may change dimensions and labels; the fingerprint, not the label, identifies a dimension.

**Assessment against the requirements (graph-derived basis, row M).** R1: the best fit to "minimum" in spirit, but the number is whatever the data and the chosen separation degree give (measured: no eigengap; NMF error 0.17 at k = 100); R2: pass in the owner's after-the-fact sense, with measured nameability comparable to the existing axes; R3: pass for tier 1/2 constructions; R4: pass in principle (the graph is synset-level, so other-language senses attach by id); R5: signed methods put held-out antonyms at negative cosine in the adjective probe; unsigned (NMF) ones do not; R6: pass; R7: the first two probes are encouraging (signed spectral) and sobering (NMF, component count), and neither was run on the project's own pair tasks.

## 3. How many dimensions does lexical meaning need? What can "minimal" mean?

- **Semantic differential:** 3 factors (evaluation, potency, activity) recur across scales, concepts and (per Osgood, May, Miron 1975) cultures [V-s]. This is the lowest-dimensional claim and it is about affective/connotative meaning, not denotation: it cannot distinguish dog from cat.
- **Hebart et al. (2020):** 49 reproducible, human-nameable dimensions explain most explainable variance in similarity judgements of 1,854 objects [V]. Object concepts only.
- **Binder et al. (2016):** ~65 hand-chosen experiential attributes [V]; **Lancaster:** 11 sensorimotor dimensions [V]; **VAD:** 3.
- **Feature norms:** McRae norms need thousands of distinct features (2,526 reported) to cover 541 concepts [V-s]: the property space is large and sparse.
- **Embeddings:** intrinsic dimension estimates for 300-d GloVe/word2vec of roughly 10-30 using TwoNN (GloVe ~24.8, word2vec ~24.75, fastText ~13.2; Kataiwa, Cho, Ohki 2025, arXiv:2503.02142) [V-s; single estimator, preprint]. Principal components of skip-gram embeddings align with valence/arousal/dominance, frequency and age of acquisition (Hollis & Westbury 2016 [V-s]).
- **This project:** effective dimension ~17 (participation ratio), ~46 for 90% of energy (M19.1), fidelity peaking at K ~ 80-120 named axes on the 234-axis space (M20.1); never re-measured on 607 (history s6).
- **MDS of similarity judgements:** the classical result for concrete categories is a handful to a few dozen dimensions; the Hebart 49 is the modern version of this [V]. I did not retrieve a specific older MDS paper [U].

**What "minimal" can realistically mean.** (a) Affect only: 3. (b) Object similarity as people judge it: ~50. (c) Distinctions a word-sense inventory needs (kind of thing + properties + scales): hundreds. The effective dimension of a named basis is always lower than its axis count because named axes cannot be rotated to remove redundancy (R2): so the practical target is not "smallest rank" but "every axis earns its place": pruned by leave-one-out contribution (M20.1 method), with no axis that is on ~every sense. That reading is compatible with all three families the owner's requirements favour (A, B, H).

## 6. The most promising candidates (stated neutrally; no winner)

**Candidate 1: Clean up and keep (A, plus the minimality re-run).** Prune/re-weight the ubiquitous axes inside the stored space, re-run honest minimality on 607 axes, keep antonymy as a relation, make the scorer's antonym consult sense-level and complete.
*Right choice if:* (a) zeroing the top-k ubiquitous axes already moves unrelated-pair similarity down and makes dog/cat vs eat/chew behave (probe A1); (b) the antonym store covers the pairs the scorer meets (probe A3: most scorer-relevant opposites are in the store); (c) the owner holds that opposition is a relation (as the history concludes) and R5 is a preference, not a rule.

**Candidate 2: Signed senses on the existing attribute scales, signs from WordNet pointers (B2).** Additive signed layer or v2 of the sense matrix; data-derived orientation; no new axes.
*Right choice if:* (a) the data rule reproduces the 130 cue-list signs and extends signs to many more adjective senses (probe B1); (b) removing the generic axes (Candidate 1) shows that sign flips then move antonym cosines substantially (otherwise the 0.04-sized effect of the cue list repeats); (c) the owner accepts signed senses (O4) and that most evaluative opposites (good/bad, happy/unhappy) may lack `attribute` pointers and so remain relation-only.

**Candidate 3: WordNet-cluster scale axes, merged (C, WordNet-only).** One two-ended axis per merged adjective cluster, senses placed with signs from head/satellite membership, built as an extra named block next to the existing one.
*Right choice if:* (a) merging the 1,999 head pairs yields a few hundred coherent axes that cover most in-cluster senses (probe C1; a long tail of tiny clusters would argue against); (b) held-out adjective antonym discrimination clearly beats Candidates 1-2 (probe C4); (c) the owner allows a flat count of several hundred scale axes as long as each sense activates few; (d) pole orientation convention and complementary/converse exceptions are acceptable as documented limits.

**Candidate 4: Typed hybrid with rated or taxonomy scales where WordNet has none (H, with E and/or D).** Blocks for categories, scales, operators; rated norms (e.g., valence) as the signed scale for evaluative vocabulary that lacks `attribute` pointers; taxonomy ancestors only for nouns/verbs.
*Right choice if:* (a) block-wise scratch comparison (probe H1) shows a combiner that fixes opposites, unrelated pairs and dog/cat vs eat/chew simultaneously, which no single block does; (b) the owner accepts human-rated data (O1) and a licence that blocks redistribution of derived coordinates or finds a permissively licensed substitute; (c) the interface change (blocks instead of one vector) is acceptable to the encoder, decoder and memory model, which currently consume one flat vector.

**Candidate 5: A sememe-style closed inventory for content words, with per-sense definition trees (S, 2b.1b), primes kept for closed-class/operator words.**
*Right choice if:* (a) a deterministic mapping from WordNet (and, through synset ids, other-language) senses to HowNet senses or to sememe-style definitions reaches most of the 117,659 senses (the released BabelSememe covers ~10% by my measurement; word-level lemma overlap is ~56% of synsets); (b) the relation-labelled tree (agent, patient, modifier, degree...) is kept as additional named coordinates, or a flat sememe set is shown to be enough on the pair tasks; (c) the sememe antonym/converse relations and taxonomy are used so that hot/cold are not simply disjoint and eat/chew are not unrelated; (d) the owner is content with `neg` appearing in lexicalised negatives (unhappy) or the definitions are re-expressed without it; (e) the licence permits shipping derived coordinates; (f) a held-out evaluation beats the current space (no English evaluation of sememe vectors on antonym or similarity benchmarks was found). Origin language is treated as neutral (owner input, 2026-11-15).

**Candidate 6: A graph-derived minimal basis, computed from the relation graph (WordNet, optionally the sememe graph) with antonym edges as negative edges, then labelled afterwards (M, 2b.7).**
*Right choice if:* (a) the construction is closed-form or convex and seeded (signed-Laplacian eigenvectors, a fixed-seed NMF or a greedy basis selection over a fixed matrix), so it is reproducible and falls on the allowed side of the line drawn in 2b.7; (b) the number of dimensions can be chosen from the data (an eigengap, a reconstruction-error knee, or a required pair-separation degree) and is far below 607 while holding the pair tasks; (c) the dimensions are nameable enough that after-the-fact labelling (top-loading senses, validated by intrusion tests, an LLM as assistant labeller) gives labels people agree on; (d) the many disconnected parts of the graph (4,809 connected components in the adjective graph alone, measured) are handled, because a spectral basis spends dimensions on components; (e) signed edges reproduce held-out antonym pairs as negative without hurting synonym/hypernym tasks; (f) the owner accepts that the definition is the construction and the labels are documentation, and that labels may change when the basis is rebuilt.

Excluded as primary candidates: G (fails R2/R3 by its construction and repeats closed doors M21b/M25), F in full (hand-explication scale), and learned geometric versions of E (R2/R3).

## 8b. Why a deterministic, named, dictionary-derived space tends to be close but not perfect

What the literature and the measurements here identify as recurring hard problems for ANY such scheme (the project's, HowNet's, Roget's, a graph-derived basis), and which of the project's observed issues each one explains. "Explains" is my reading (INFERENCE) unless a source is cited.

1. **Ubiquitous / generic features dominate common-feature similarity.** Tversky's contrast model (1977 [V]) makes similarity a weighted sum of common features minus distinctive ones; with equal weights, widely shared features dominate, and Smith, Shoben and Rips (1974 [V]) already separated defining from characteristic features because shared generic features are poor discriminators. Dictionary definitions are built on generic genus terms ("a thing that ...", "a person who ..."), so any space derived from glosses inherits them. HowNet escapes the worst of it (most frequent sememe on 9.3% of senses, measured) only because its definitions are written against a specific inventory, at the cost of resolution (35,119 distinct DEF strings for 220,308 senses). *Explains:* SOMETHING/ONE/PART on ~98% of senses; random-pair cosine floor ~0.28-0.40 (measured 0.40 on raw sense vectors); "cosine is substantially a genericity match" (M42).
2. **Opposites share everything except one thing (minimal difference).** Murphy (2003) and Jones et al. (2012) [V-s] record the long-standing minimal-difference principle for antonym pairs; Justeson & Katz (1991 [V]) show antonyms co-occur about ten times more often than chance, so distributional models also place them close; Mohammad et al. (2013 [V]) note that contrast is poorly captured by relatedness. Any space whose job is to preserve similarity will therefore place opposites close unless a feature or relation is dedicated to the difference. HowNet shows the opposite failure in flat use (hot/cold = disjoint sememes = similarity 0). *Explains:* happy/unhappy 0.89, happy/sad 0.85, and why five attempts (history s2.3) gave at most 0.69-0.78 (an improvement, not a fix).
3. **Opposition is not one relation.** Cruse's typology (complementaries, polar/overlapping/equipollent antonyms, directional opposites, reversives, converses [V-s]) and Kennedy & McNally's open/closed scales [V] mean a sign on one axis fits some kinds only. HowNet needs separate `antonym` (241 pairs) and `converse` (114 pairs) relations and a `neg` operator; WordNet's verb antonym pointers are mostly reversives/complementaries (measured). *Explains:* why "antonyms are more complicated than one dimension" (owner), the low coverage of sign-based fixes (attr axes: 8% of words), and the verb gap (only 5 held-out v-v antonym pairs, history s1).
4. **Similarity versus relatedness are different relations.** SimLex-999 (2015 [V]) was built to separate them; association data (SWOW, USF [V]) mix them; taxonomy-derived measures (Resnik, Wu-Palmer [V]) favour nouns because noun hierarchies are deep (mean depth 8.5) and verb hierarchies are flat (mean 2.5; measured). *Explains:* dog/cat closer than eat/chew (Wu-Palmer 0.857 vs 0.20; HowNet: eat and chew share no sememe).
5. **Verbs mean differently from nouns and adjectives.** Verb meaning lives in event structure, manner/result, causation, aspect and argument structure (Vendler 1957, Dowty 1991, Levin & Rappaport Hovav 2005, Rappaport Hovav & Levin 2010 [V/V-s]); WordNet organises verbs by troponymy with 996 antonym, 390 entailment and 218 cause pointers (measured). Scale axes and noun-style categories give little there. *Explains:* the verb weakness for antonym- and taxonomy-based options.
6. **Polysemy and granularity of the sense inventory.** WordNet's sense distinctions are fine; HowNet's are coarser (sheep = horse = pig; 6.3 senses per DEF); lemma-level relations (antonym pointers, propagation edges) mix senses, and the project's core coordinates are word-level mixtures. *Explains:* "antonyms('hot') includes 'bad'" (M29), the low gain of word-level signs on senses, and the WSD ceiling (M30).
7. **Gradation needs an anchored, ordered scale.** Gradable adjectives need a scale with a standard of comparison (Kennedy & McNally [V]); WordNet has no intensity ordering (Sheinman et al. 2013 added one from the web [V]); HowNet gives degree only through relations and distinct value sememes. *Explains:* no sign at the sense level and no ordering for big/large/huge in the current space.
8. **No agreed primitive inventory, and no proof that one exists.** Fodor et al. (1980 [V]) argue against definitions; Katz & Fodor's distinguishers were never systematised [V-s]; NSM has 65 primes by cross-linguistic study [V-s]; HowNet has about 2,000-2,500 and defines some sememes through others; intrinsic-dimension estimates of embeddings are 10-30 (one preprint) while the project's own curve peaks at 80-120 named axes. A named inventory is therefore a design choice, not a discovery. *Explains:* 607 axes against ~17 effective dimensions, and the unresolved meaning of "minimum".
9. **Dictionary-derived spaces are circular and incomplete at the edges.** Glosses define words by other words; closed-class and highly abstract words are defined poorly or not at all (the stopword exclusion that left 26 prime axes empty); rare or technical items fall back to weaker grounding (1,208 senses, 1.0%). *Explains:* empty prime axes and the fallback senses.
10. **Hand data versus derived data trade coverage for precision.** Hand-built inventories (HowNet, Roget, Cyc, VerbCorner) are precise and sparse (BabelSememe maps ~10% of WordNet; norms cover thousands of words); fully derived spaces cover everything and blur (the 607-axis space). *Explains:* the project's position in the middle (derived from WordNet's own hand-built relations) and why every alternative costs either coverage or sharpness.
11. **Fixed-geometry comparison.** A single cosine over all axes cannot weight common, distinctive, and relational evidence differently (Tversky's point), and a metric cannot be asymmetric (hypernymy, "North Korea is like China") [V-s]. *Explains:* hypernym AUC stuck at ~0.72 (M19.4 onward) and why directional structure went to the relation store (M28.1).

**Mapping of the project's observed issues to causes (summary).**

| Observed issue | Main causes above |
|---|---|
| opposites nearly identical (happy/unhappy 0.89, happy/sad 0.85) | 2, 3, 1 (shared generic mass hides the one difference), 7 |
| unrelated words not well separated | 1, 9 |
| dog/cat closer than eat/chew | 4, 5, 6 |
| ubiquitous axes on ~98% of senses | 1, 9 |
| signs only for a small core and dropped at the sense level | 3, 7, 6 (word-level vs sense-level), 10 |
| 607 axes versus ~17 effective dimensions | 8, 10 |
| hypernym AUC flat (~0.72) | 11, 4 |
| verbs weak for antonym- and taxonomy-based fixes | 5, 3 |

A practical reading: items 1, 2 and 11 are partly addressable by how similarity is computed (weights, relation consultation, asymmetry) without changing the coordinates; items 3, 5, 6 and 7 need different kinds of structure (typed blocks, relations) rather than more axes; items 8-10 are choices the owner can make but cannot "solve".
